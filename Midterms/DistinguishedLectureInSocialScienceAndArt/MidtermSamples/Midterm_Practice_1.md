## Answers B. Suggested written responses

### Q21. Suggested response

**Source:** W1 s10-17.

**Notes:** [Authority](../notes/01_Week1_CriticalThinking.md#3-authority-and-independent-judgment-slides-10-12), [development](../notes/01_Week1_CriticalThinking.md#5-cognitive-development-slide-15).

The statement resembles **dualism**: authority is presumed to know the answer and questioning is ruled out. Commitment examines evidence and takes a justified position while remaining open to revision. Ask for the professor's supporting reasons, relevant expertise and treatment of objections. The conclusion may be correct, but the classmate's refusal to examine support does not establish it.

### Q22. Suggested response

**Source:** W5 s6-9. The front-row example is in s8.

**Notes:** [Front-row reconstruction](../notes/05_Week5_Arguments.md#6-front-row-example-slide-8), [evaluation](../notes/05_Week5_Arguments.md#7-five-evaluation-criteria-slide-9).

- **Premise 1:** front-row students generally have higher grades.
- **Premise 2:** the listener wants to improve their GPA.
- **Conclusion:** the listener should move forward.
- **Hidden premise:** moving forward helps cause improvement and is a feasible way to pursue it.
- **Clarity:** define generally and the relevant student group.
- **Credibility:** inspect evidence for the observed relationship.
- **Relevance:** the observation matters, but does not establish the causal recommendation by itself.
- **Completeness:** the causal and practical premises are unstated.
- **Soundness/support:** the stated premises alone do not establish that moving this student will raise their GPA.

Motivation or attendance could influence both seating and grades. A suitable comparative study that addresses these alternatives would strengthen a causal recommendation. This criticism does not prove seating has no effect.

### Q23. Suggested response

**Source:** W6.1 s5, W4 s12,17.

**Notes:** [Ad analysis](../notes/06_Week6_Marketing_Advertising.md#5-worked-advertisement-analysis), [fallacy reference](../Fallacy_Reference.md).

The conclusion is to buy the pen expecting a grade increase. **Snob appeal** substitutes elite status for performance evidence. **Questionable cause** attributes the customer's improvement to the purchase without excluding changes in study effort or other factors. **Hasty generalization** extends one experience to other buyers. Obtain relevant comparisons and report the evidence's limits. A narrow, accurately attributed testimonial would describe that customer's report without promising everyone an improvement.

### Q24. Suggested response

**Source:** W7 s7-10.

**Notes:** [Method](../notes/08_Week7_Science.md#4-scientific-method-slide-7), [hypotheses](../notes/08_Week7_Science.md#5-evaluating-hypotheses-slide-8), [ethics](../notes/08_Week7_Science.md#8-ethics-and-pressures-slide-10).

1. Identify the problem: whether reminders improve on-time completion.
2. Hypothesize higher completion in the reminder condition.
3. Define reminder, completion window and comparison conditions, then refine the design.
4. Test with a suitable comparison, consistent measurements and ethical participation.
5. Evaluate outcomes, uncertainty and alternatives, then revise the explanation if needed.

The hypothesis is **relevant** to completion. Check **consistency** with well-established knowledge rather than simply asserting it. Prefer a **simple adequate** explanation without hiding alternative causes. It is **testable/falsifiable** if outcomes can count against the predicted improvement. It has **predictive content** if it specifies what should happen in comparable conditions. None of these criteria supplies a result before data collection.

For collection, address informed consent and respect for participants. For reporting, disclose unfavorable results honestly and cite sources. This is a proposed study, not evidence that reminders already work.
