## Answers B. Suggested written responses

### Q21. Suggested response

**Source:** W3 s7-10.

**Notes:** [Confirmation bias](../notes/03_Week3_Knowledge_Evidence_Biases.md#4-confirmation-bias-slide-7), [social errors](../notes/03_Week3_Knowledge_Evidence_Biases.md#7-social-errors-and-biases-slide-10).

| Mechanism | Evidence in the scenario | Targeted response |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation bias | Collect only favorable reviews | Seek counterevidence and apply equal standards |
| Group pressure/groupthink | Suppress doubts to preserve agreement | Invite independent assessments and objections |
| Memorable-events error | A vivid positive story displaces broader evidence | Compare it with systematic records and relevant frequencies |
| Diffusion of responsibility | Everyone assumes another person will report the bug | Assign a named reporting owner and confirm the report |

Agreement can follow careful evaluation. Here, suppressing scrutiny to maintain agreement is the reason for the groupthink diagnosis. Do not infer it from consensus alone.

### Q22. Suggested response

**Source:** W4 s10-12.

**Notes:** [Relevance fallacies](../notes/04_Week4_InformalFallacies.md#4-relevance-slides-9-13).

Conclusion: the rumor is true. The first offered reason, absence of disproof, commits **appeal to ignorance**. The second, widespread belief, commits **popular appeal**. The third attacks dissenters' character instead of their evidence, an **ad hominem** diversion from the claim's support.

Trace the rumor to its original source, seek relevant corroboration and counterevidence, and distinguish uncertainty from proof. The diagnosis does not establish falsity: the rumor could be true, false or unresolved, but these reasons do not settle it.

### Q23. Suggested response

**Source:** W6.2 s5-7, W3 s5-7, W7 s9.

**Notes:** [Scientific reporting](../notes/07_Week6_MassMedia.md#2-evaluating-scientific-reporting-slide-5), [worked media case](../notes/07_Week6_MassMedia.md#5-worked-report-analysis).

The six reporting checks ask who makes the claim, source credentials/funding, reporter background, original publication, response from other relevant authorities and possible bias. The scenario identifies funding but leaves several checks unresolved. Obtain the original report and methodological details before endorsing the headline.

Limitations include a self-selected sample of existing users, no stated comparison that isolates the app's causal contribution, and a universal guarantee stronger than the described evidence. These criticisms do not prove the app has no benefit. Funding suggests a possible incentive requiring scrutiny, not proof that every finding is false.

For media literacy, identify the creator and purpose, the anxiety-inducing image and urgency, and the emphasis on academic success. Explain how anxious students might react differently from skeptical readers. Check facts and reasoning before predicting possible effects such as premature adoption or uncritical sharing. The immediate emotional reaction should itself be examined.

### Q24. Suggested response

**Source:** W7 s5,8-11.

**Notes:** [Designs](../notes/08_Week7_Science.md#7-research-methodology-and-designs-slide-9), [hypotheses](../notes/08_Week7_Science.md#5-evaluating-hypotheses-slide-8), [Kuhn](../notes/08_Week7_Science.md#9-kuhn-and-scientific-paradigms-slide-11).

The first approach is **correlational**: it measures naturally occurring association. Motivation or prior preparation could contribute to both study time and grades. The second is **experimental** if it intervenes and uses a suitable controlled comparison. Intervention by itself is not sufficient; inspect group comparability, measurement and competing explanations.

For **testability/falsifiability**, specify an outcome that could count against the predicted relationship. For **relevance**, ensure the hypothesis addresses the actual question about study time and grades. Prediction, consistency and simplicity can also be assessed with justification.

A surprising result need not replace a paradigm. **Normal science** can investigate unexpected findings within accepted methods. A **scientific revolution**, in the lecture's Kuhnian account, involves replacing a problematic paradigm with a new theory. Collection should respect informed consent and participants; reporting should honestly include outcomes and properly cite sources.
