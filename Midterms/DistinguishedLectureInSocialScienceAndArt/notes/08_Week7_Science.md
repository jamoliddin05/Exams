# Week 7: Science

What this lecture is about: scientific assumptions and limitations, methods and hypothesis evaluation, research designs, ethics and scientific paradigms.

Source: `DLSSA 7.pptx`, 11 slides, Chapter 12. Slide 2 lists global warming, Covid, nuclear energy, AI and vaccines as discussion topics and includes a climate illustration. These notes do not treat that image as a verified current forecast or supply medical advice.

## 1. Science and underlying assumptions (slide 4)

**Science:** using reason to move from observable, measurable facts to hypotheses and testable explanations.

| Assumption named in the lecture | Lecture meaning |
|---|---|
| Empiricism | Physical senses are the primary source of knowledge |
| Objectivity | The physical world can be observed and studied without observer bias |
| Materialism | Everything in the universe consists of physical matter |
| Predictability | The universe contains interconnected causal relationships |
| Unity | The universe has an underlying unified dynamic structure |

**Study clarification:** this table reproduces the lecture's philosophical framing. Objectivity is a methodological aim, not a guarantee that each scientist or measurement is bias-free. Check procedures and evidence.

## 2. Limitations (slide 5)

The slide raises these issues:

- Descartes' question about having ideas of an external world rather than direct proof of it.
- Empirical inquiry's dependence on what can be observed.
- Observation alone cannot logically establish a necessary causal connection.
- Experience depends on our brains.
- Quantum mechanics, listed without further explanation.

**Study implication:** distinguish an observation from an interpretation and a causal inference. Limits of observation do not establish that every claim is equally well supported.

No quantum-mechanical mechanism or numerical result is supplied, so none is reconstructed here.

## 3. Science and religion (slide 6)

Four positions listed in the course:

1. Science takes priority when a conflict arises.
2. Religion takes priority when a conflict arises.

## 4. Scientific method (slide 7)

**Scientific method:** rigorous, systematic application of observation and experimentation.

The lecture's five steps:

1. Identify the problem.
2. Develop an initial hypothesis or explanation.
3. Gather more information and refine the hypothesis.
4. Test it.
5. Evaluate it using the testing or experimental results.

**Source wording caution:** the slide places scientific theory in parentheses beside initial hypothesis. For study purposes, an initial proposed explanation remains a hypothesis requiring evaluation. Do not infer from this slide that any guess is already an established scientific theory.

New study example:

| Step | Application |
|---|---|
| Problem | Does a reminder improve on-time practice completion? |
| Hypothesis | Students receiving a reminder complete practice on time more often |
| Refinement | Define reminder timing, completion and the comparison group |
| Test | Use a suitable comparative design and record completion consistently |
| Evaluation | Compare outcomes, examine uncertainty and competing explanations, revise as needed |

The example has no invented results. The design must address ethics as well as inference.

## 5. Evaluating hypotheses (slide 8)

Five criteria:

| Criterion | Evaluation question |
|---|---|
| Relevance | Does it address the problem? |
| Consistency | Is it consistent with well-established theories? |
| Simplicity | Is it the simplest adequate explanation? |
| Testability and falsifiability | Could observations test it and potentially count against it? |
| Prediction | Does it predict the outcomes of similar events? |

**Study clarification:** simplicity alone does not establish truth. Conflicting results require checking methods and assumptions, and may motivate revision. Consistency with existing theories is a criterion for evaluation, not a prohibition on testing theories themselves.

**Study contrast:** "The reminder effect occurs only when nobody can observe it, and every possible result confirms it" blocks testing against the claim. A hypothesis that specifies a measurable comparison can face counterevidence.

## 6. Pseudoscience (slide 8)

**Pseudoscience:** explanations or hypotheses presented as science but grounded in emotional appeals, superstition or rhetoric. The lecture lists astrology, psychic healing, numerology and mind reading as examples.

In an analysis, identify the actual failure of evidence or testability rather than relying only on an insulting label. A failed prediction is a reason to reassess a hypothesis, not automatically evidence of deliberate deception.

## 7. Research methodology and designs (slide 9)

**Research methodology:** a systematic approach to gathering and analyzing information.

The slide lists the following eight designs. It does not define them fully. The distinctions and examples below are **study clarifications**:

| Design named in lecture | Study distinction | New study example |
|---|---|---|
| Experimental | Intervene and compare outcomes under a controlled design | Compare reminder versus no-reminder conditions |
| Correlational | Examine associations without the intervention needed to isolate causation | Compare naturally occurring study time and grades |
| Survey | Collect responses using questions | Ask students about study habits |
| Ethnographic | Study practices and meanings within a social setting | Observe routines in a student project community |
| Grounded theory | Develop an explanatory account iteratively from collected data | Refine a model of how students seek help using interviews |
| Narrative | Examine accounts and stories of experience | Analyze a student's account of their first university year |
| Action | Investigate a practical change through cycles of action and reflection | Revise a classroom routine, observe it and revise again |
| Mixed methods | Combine qualitative and quantitative approaches | Relate completion counts to interview accounts of motivation |

The lecture gives intervention, simulation and laboratory work as experimental examples. Design labels can overlap: a project may use a survey within a mixed-method study. Name the feature that justifies the classification.

## 8. Ethics and pressures (slide 10)

The lecture includes informed consent and respect for sites, participants and norms in **data collection**. For **data reporting**, it names honesty, proper citation and timely publication.

It also raises publish-or-perish pressure and concerns about scientific neutrality.

**Study application:** do not fabricate favorable outcomes or omit inconvenient data to meet a publication deadline. Participation and data handling require ethical consideration even when a design is methodologically strong. A useful answer separates collection risks from reporting risks.

## 9. Kuhn and scientific paradigms (slide 11)

| Term | Lecture meaning |
|---|---|
| Normal science | Research grounded in past achievements recognized by most scientists |
| Paradigm | Accepted view of the world and how to study it |
| Scientific revolution | A paradigm shift in which a new theory replaces a problematic paradigm |

The slide presents Kuhn as challenging an uncomplicated account of scientific objectivity and progress. **Study clarification:** a paradigm concerns accepted questions, concepts and methods, not merely any individual hypothesis. Not every result that surprises one researcher is a scientific revolution.

- Explain testability and what evidence could count against a claim.

- Separate ethical issues in collection and reporting.
