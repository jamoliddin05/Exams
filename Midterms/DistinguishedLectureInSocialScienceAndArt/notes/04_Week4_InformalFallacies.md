# Week 4: Informal Fallacies

What this lecture is about: recognizing faulty reasoning through ambiguity, irrelevance and unsupported assumptions, then improving an argument.

Source: `DLSSA 4.pptx`, 19 slides, Chapter 5. Slide 2 names Shannon Townsend without describing the case. Slide 19 assigns a comparison of linked Iraq-war readings whose text is not in the deck.

## 1. Fallacies and argument form (slides 4-5)

**Fallacy:** a faulty argument that initially appears correct.

**Formal fallacy:** the argument's form is invalid. The lecture's example says some school dropouts are men, no doctors are dropouts, therefore no doctors are men.

**Study explanation:** the premises allow a male doctor who is not a dropout. That possibility makes the inference invalid, regardless of whether someone finds its wording persuasive.

**Informal fallacy:** reasoning that can persuade psychologically or emotionally while failing logically. Inspect the meaning, relevance and assumptions of its content.

The course groups informal fallacies as **ambiguity**, **relevance** and **unwarranted assumptions**. Preserve this classification even if another book arranges some names differently.

## 2. How to identify a fallacy

Study procedure:

1. State the conclusion and premises.
2. Identify the exact step that fails to support the conclusion.
3. Name the best-fitting lecture category and fallacy.
4. Explain why the mechanism fits this wording.
5. Repair it by clarifying terms, supplying evidence or narrowing the conclusion.

An unsupported inference does not prove its conclusion false. It establishes a problem with the offered support.

## 3. Ambiguity (slides 6-8)

| Fallacy | Lecture mechanism | Lecture example, paraphrased |
|---|---|---|
| Equivocation | A key term changes meaning | Interpret a restaurant's no-animals rule as excluding humans because humans are biologically animals |
| Amphiboly | Grammar permits competing interpretations | Whose husband is meant in the sentence about Mary's mother and her husband? |
| Accent | Meaning changes with emphasis or selective presentation | Read a crackdown on off-campus drinking as approval of on-campus drinking |
| Division | Attribute a group's characteristic to each individual | Infer that a specific Canadian must be nice from a general claim about Canadians |

**Study clarification:** ambiguity in a sentence becomes a reasoning problem when an inference relies on one unestablished reading. Ask which meaning the context actually supports.

The lecture places division in this category. Do not silently move it to another taxonomy in a course answer.

## 4. Relevance (slides 9-13)

The premise does not appropriately support the conclusion.

| Fallacy | Mechanism | New study example |
|---|---|---|
| Ad hominem | Attack a person instead of addressing their argument | Her timetable proposal is wrong because she is unpleasant |
| Appeal to force | Threaten someone to secure acceptance | Agree that my estimate is correct or lose your place in the group |
| Appeal to pity | Use pity where it is irrelevant to the claim | My week was terrible, so my calculation must be correct |
| Popular appeal | Treat popularity as proof | Everyone shares this post, so its claim must be true |
| Appeal to ignorance | Treat lack of disproof as proof, or lack of proof as disproof | Nobody disproved this rumor, so it is true |
| Hasty generalization | Generalize from an insufficient or biased sample | Two laptops from the brand failed, so every laptop it sells is defective |
| Straw man | Replace an opponent's position with an easier target | A request for a small quiz revision becomes a demand to abolish all assessment |
| Red herring | Divert discussion to a different conclusion | Asked about missing evidence, answer by praising the team's popularity |

**Contrasts:** straw man misrepresents the position being opposed. Red herring redirects attention away from the issue. Popular appeal concerns numbers or social acceptance, while relevant expertise concerns reasons to trust a source in its field.

Not every emotional appeal is irrelevant: suffering can matter to a policy about relief. The failure occurs when the emotion does not support the particular conclusion.

## 5. Unwarranted assumptions (slides 14-17)

| Fallacy | Unsupported assumption | New study example |
|---|---|---|
| Begging the question | The conclusion is already assumed in the premises | This rule is fair because it is a just rule |
| Inappropriate appeal to authority | Authority outside the relevant field establishes truth | A famous singer endorses a claim about a processor, so the claim must be true |
| Loaded question | An unestablished claim is built into the question | When did you stop falsifying the group results? |
| False dilemma | The stated alternatives exhaust all possibilities | Either accept my entire plan or you oppose improving the course |
| Questionable cause, including post hoc | A causal connection exists without sufficient support | My lucky shirt preceded a good grade, so it caused the grade |
| Slippery slope | An initial step inevitably permits an extreme chain | One deadline extension means soon no deadlines will exist |
| Naturalistic fallacy | Natural origin establishes goodness | This ingredient is natural, so it must be good |

Slide 15's "rewarding" in the begging-the-question definition appears to mean **rewording**. The useful distinction is circular support rather than an independent reason.

**Course-specific naturalistic fallacy:** use the natural-implies-good definition above. This is the formulation taught here.

A supported causal argument or documented chain of consequences is not automatically fallacious. Examine evidence for the links.

## 6. Closely related errors

| Pair | Distinguishing question |
|---|---|
| Equivocation and amphiboly | Is the problem a word's shifting meaning or a sentence's grammar? |
| Division and hasty generalization | Does reasoning move from group to individual, or from a small sample to a wider group? |
| Begging the question and loaded question | Is circular support offered, or is an unproved premise built into the question? |
| Questionable cause and slippery slope | Is one causal link asserted, or an unsupported chain to an extreme result? |
| Popular appeal and snob appeal | Is acceptance sought through widespread popularity or exclusive status? Snob appeal appears in week 6.1 |

## 7. Avoiding fallacies (slide 18)

The lecture recommends knowing oneself, confidence and self-esteem, listening well, clear language and grammar, separating argument quality from a speaker's character, knowing the topic, skepticism, attention to body language and giving up the need to win.

**Study application:** replace "you are wrong because you are lazy" with a direct examination of the premise, evidence and inference. Personal confidence and good presentation help discussion but do not make a weak premise true.

## 8. External comparison task (slide 19)

The lecture asks students to compare claims in two linked Iraq-war readings. The deck does not supply their full arguments, so these notes make no verdict about which author has the stronger argument.

For the task, reconstruct each argument fairly, identify quoted evidence in the actual reading, distinguish a false claim from a faulty inference, and justify each proposed fallacy classification. Political disagreement alone is insufficient to diagnose a fallacy.

## Exam focus

- Name all three categories and place the 19 lecture fallacies in them.
- Identify the conclusion before assigning a label.
- Explain a fallacy's mechanism in the particular example.
- Distinguish similar labels using the table above.
- Give a counterexample to an invalid inference or suggest the evidence it needs.
- Explain why a fallacious argument can still have a true conclusion.
