# Week 3: Knowledge, Evidence and Errors in Thinking

What this lecture is about: justification for knowledge claims, credibility of evidence and experts, and cognitive and social sources of error.

Source: `DLSSA 3.pptx`, 10 slides, Chapter 4. Slide 2 names Peter Reilly's case without providing the narrative. Do not infer case details or a legal conclusion from the title and photograph.

## 1. Knowledge and its limitations (slide 4)

**Knowledge, in the lecture:** information or experience believed to be true and supported by justification or evidence.

| Position | Lecture emphasis |
|---|---|
| Rationalists | Most knowledge comes through reason |
| Empiricists | Truth is discovered mainly through physical senses and empirical evidence |
| Kant and current psychologists, as summarized here | The mind supplies structure and rules for processing incoming information |

These are brief contrasts, not complete accounts of each philosophical position. Evidence can require both observation and reasoning about what an observation means.

**Study example:** seeing a measurement is an observation. Deciding whether it supports a general claim also requires interpretation, assumptions and reasoning.

## 2. Evidence and testimony (slide 5)

**Evidence:** something that tends to prove or disprove a claim. A piece of evidence need not settle the claim by itself.

| Type or risk | Lecture meaning | Study example and check |
|---|---|---|
| Direct experience | Personally encountered evidence | You saw a device fail. Check conditions and repeatability before generalizing |
| False memory syndrome | Recalling events that never happened | Confidence in a memory does not substitute for independent records |
| Hearsay | Information heard by one person and repeated to another | Trace "a friend heard that..." to its original source |
| Anecdotal evidence | Personal testimonies | One satisfied customer is evidence about that experience, not a representative sample of all customers |

The label false memory syndrome is used as in the slide. These notes use it for a reasoning risk and make no clinical diagnosis.

**Study clarification:** direct experience can be mistaken, and testimony can be credible. Evaluate how evidence was obtained rather than automatically ranking every first-person account above every reported study.

## 3. Experts and credibility (slide 6)

The lecture lists four credentials: **education, experience, reputation and accomplishments**. It also asks whether expert testimony is foolproof.

**Study answer:** no. Check expertise in the relevant field and whether the particular claim has supporting evidence. Credentials help assess credibility but do not guarantee every statement is true.

**Study example:** a skilled actor's fame does not establish expertise about a laboratory measurement. A relevant specialist should still explain their method and uncertainty.

Compare [inappropriate appeal to authority](04_Week4_InformalFallacies.md#5-unwarranted-assumptions-slides-14-17). Consulting a qualified expert is not automatically fallacious.

## 4. Confirmation bias (slide 7)

**Confirmation bias:** looking only for evidence that supports one's assumptions.

Study example:

| Step | Biased approach | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Initial belief | My preferred app is always more reliable | State a claim that could be checked |
| Search | Collect only positive reviews | Seek failures and comparisons as well |
| Evaluation | Dismiss every negative result as unfair | Apply the same standards to both sides |

Rejecting weak counterevidence can be justified. The bias occurs when standards depend on whether evidence agrees with the initial belief.

## 5. Perception, randomness and probability (slide 8)

| Error | Lecture focus | Study example |
|---|---|---|
| Perceptual error | Perception can mislead | A visual impression needs measurement or another check |
| Misperception of random data | Random patterns may look meaningful | A short streak does not itself establish a predictive pattern |
| Memorable-events error | Vivid outstanding events dominate memory | One dramatic failure crowds out many routine outcomes |
| Probability error | Greatly misjudging an event's chances | Assuming a vivid possibility must also be likely |

The slide links an optical-illusion site. Its individual demonstrations are not reproduced here. The examples above explain the listed mechanisms without assigning invented experimental results.

## 6. Self-serving biases and prophecy (slide 9)

The lecture lists perceived control, overestimating oneself relative to others, and exaggerating strengths while minimizing weaknesses.

**Self-fulfilling prophecy:** distorted or exaggerated expectations reinforce actions that help bring about the expected event.

**Study example:** a student expects rejection, never asks teammates for help and then receives little help. The expectation changes behavior, which contributes to the result.

Distinguish this from confirmation bias: selecting friendly evidence changes what one notices; a self-fulfilling prophecy changes behavior in a way that helps produce an outcome. Both can occur together.

## 7. Social errors and biases (slide 10)

| Lecture item | Study clarification | Study example |
|---|---|---|
| One of us/one of them | Judge a claim through group membership | Trust a teammate's unsupported claim while rejecting the same claim from another group |
| Societal expectations and stereotypes | Assume a person matches a social category | Infer skill from a demographic label |
| Group pressure and conformity, including groupthink | Agreement pressure suppresses scrutiny | A committee avoids raising objections to maintain consensus |
| Diffusion of responsibility | Treat the problem as someone else's responsibility in a large group | Everyone sees an error and assumes someone else will report it |

Consensus alone does not establish groupthink. Ask whether members considered objections and evaluated evidence.

## 8. Evaluating an evidence-based claim

Study procedure combining slides 4-10:

1. Specify the claim and what evidence would count for or against it.
2. Locate the original observation, record, testimony or study.
3. Check relevant expertise and method.
4. Identify limits, sampling issues and alternative explanations.
5. Look deliberately for counterevidence and biased selection.
6. State a conclusion proportional to the evidence.

## Exam focus

- Define knowledge, evidence, hearsay, anecdote and confirmation bias.
- Name the four expert credentials and explain their limits.
- Distinguish memorable-events error from probability error.
- Distinguish confirmation bias, self-serving bias and self-fulfilling prophecy.
- Distinguish groupthink from diffusion of responsibility.
- For a scenario, identify the mechanism and a concrete way to reduce the error.
