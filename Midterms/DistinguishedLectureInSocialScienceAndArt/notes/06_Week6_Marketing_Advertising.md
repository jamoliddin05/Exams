# Week 6.1: Marketing and Advertising

What this lecture is about: marketing research, advertising purposes and critically evaluating persuasive commercial messages.

Source: `DLSSA 6.1.pptx`, 5 slides, labeled Chapter 10. W1's schedule groups marketing with media and politics under Chapter 11. These notes follow the actual supplied deck.

## 1. Marketing research and strategy (slide 3)

The lecture describes marketing as an essential part of business in a consumer culture. **Marketing research** identifies a target market and examines whether a product matches customer desires.

| Method | Lecture emphasis | Study example |
|---|---|---|
| Survey | Collect information | Ask students which features they need |
| Observation | Monitor buying patterns | Record how shoppers choose between shelf positions |
| Experimentation | Measure cause-effect relationships | Vary a display while keeping relevant conditions comparable |

**Strategic plan:** a method for deploying organizational resources to achieve a goal. The slide names brand recognition, motivating impulse buying and lengthening the purchase experience as marketing considerations.

**Study distinction:** finding that two behaviors occur together is not enough to demonstrate that one caused the other. A proposed experiment needs a credible comparison and control of competing explanations.

## 2. Advertising and product placement (slide 4)

Three purposes in the lecture:

1. Create product awareness.
2. Inform people about a product or service.
3. Motivate customer demand.

**Product placement:** a real commercial product appears within fictional or other media or sports. The lecture notes that the placement may go unnoticed.

**Study example:** a recognizable device repeatedly appears in a drama. Its presence can increase recognition without supplying evidence that its technical claims are true.

Marketing includes research and strategy. Advertising is a way to communicate and motivate demand within that broader activity.

## 3. Fallacies in advertisements (slide 5)

The lecture lists these recurring devices. Mechanisms and examples below are study explanations using the definitions from [week 4](04_Week4_InformalFallacies.md).

| Device | What to inspect | New study example |
|---|---|---|
| Scare tactics | Is fear replacing relevant evidence? | Buy this storage plan or your entire future is ruined |
| Questionable cause | Is an effect attributed without adequate support? | I used this pen and passed, so the pen caused success |
| Popular appeal/group pressure | Does widespread use substitute for quality evidence? | Everyone in your class owns it, so it must be best |
| Snob appeal | Does exclusive status substitute for quality evidence? | Only the elite choose it, so it must be superior |
| Inappropriate authority | Is the endorser qualified in the relevant field? | A celebrity's fame proves the device's performance |
| Hasty generalization | Is a small or biased sample generalized? | One testimonial proves it works for every buyer |
| Amphiboly | Does grammar permit a misleading reading? | A slogan leaves unclear what the claimed comparison covers |
| Naturalistic fallacy | Is natural origin treated as goodness? | Natural means automatically good |

Fear or popularity can be relevant to some decisions, but they do not establish unrelated factual claims. Explain the failed inference rather than assigning a label solely because an emotion appears.

## 4. Other persuasive techniques (slide 5)

These items are also listed but are not all inherently fallacious:

| Technique | Study clarification | Evaluation question |
|---|---|---|
| Euphemism | A milder substitution that can soften an unpleasant description | What concrete fact does the wording obscure? |
| Hyperbole | Exaggeration | Is it obvious promotional language or a factual claim needing support? |
| Analogy | A comparison used to explain or support something | Are the similarities relevant, and where does the analogy fail? |
| Logic and statistics | Numerical or inferential support | What was measured, in which sample, against what baseline, and with what inference? |

The statistical questions are study clarifications, since the slide supplies no dataset or computation. A number printed in an advertisement is not automatically false or adequate evidence.

## 5. Worked advertisement analysis

New study example: "Only top students use BrightPen. One user raised her grade after buying it. Choose BrightPen and your grades will rise."

| Element | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Target conclusion | Buy the pen expecting a grade increase |
| Offered support | Elite association and one before-after testimonial |
| Snob appeal | Top-student status is used as a reason to buy |
| Causal weakness | Other changes, including study effort, could explain the grade |
| Generalization weakness | One user's experience cannot establish the outcome for all buyers |
| Repair | Obtain relevant comparative evidence and state a claim proportional to it |

More than one weakness can occur. Tie each name to a separate piece of reasoning.

## Exam focus

- Distinguish survey, observation and experimentation.
- Define strategic plan and product placement.
- State the three advertising purposes.
- Distinguish popular appeal from snob appeal.
- Explain euphemism, hyperbole and analogy without assuming every use is a fallacy.
- Analyze an ad's claim, audience, evidence and unsupported inference.
