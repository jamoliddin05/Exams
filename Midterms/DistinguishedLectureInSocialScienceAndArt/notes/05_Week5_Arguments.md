# Week 5: Recognizing, Analyzing and Constructing Arguments

What this lecture is about: framing an issue, finding premises and conclusions, evaluating support and constructing an argument that can withstand objections.

Source: `DLSSA 5.pptx`, 10 slides, Chapter 6. Slide 2 names the Lincoln-Douglas debates without supplying debate transcripts. Worked examples are study additions unless identified as lecture examples.

## 1. Issues and rhetoric (slides 4-5)

**Issue:** an ill-defined complex of problems involving controversy or uncertainty. Define it clearly and ask the right question before arguing.

**Study example:** "AI in education" is a broad topic. "Should students be allowed to use an AI assistant to check a draft if they disclose the assistance?" is an issue with a clearer decision and scope.

The lecture contrasts **rhetoric**, defense of a position often without adequate attention to opposing evidence, with **argumentation**, reasoning directed toward discovering truth. It warns against polarizing an issue.

**Study clarification:** this is the course's contrast. Persuasive presentation and good argument can coexist. Evaluate whether the speaker considers evidence and objections, rather than rejecting any persuasive wording automatically.

## 2. Propositions, premises and conclusions (slide 6)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Proposition | A complete thought that can be true or false |
| Argument | Two or more propositions, with one supported by the others |
| Premise | A proposition offered in support of the conclusion |
| Conclusion | The proposition the argument seeks to establish |

False statements still express propositions. A question or command alone does not assert a proposition in this sense.

The lecture gives a correct astronomical statement and the false statement that Toronto is Canada's capital as examples of propositions. Their truth values differ, but both can be evaluated as true or false.

An argument is a support relationship, not simply a disagreement or a list of facts.

## 3. Four types of premise (slide 7)

| Type | Lecture description | New study example |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Based on empirical facts | The library closes at 18:00 |
| Prescriptive | Contains a value statement | Students should have equitable access to study space |
| Analogical | Compares similar things or events | A pilot at a comparable campus suggests this approach may work here |
| Definitional | Defines a key term | An anonymous survey does not record respondents' identities |

The type identifies the premise's role or content, not whether it is well supported. An analogy needs relevant similarities. A value premise should be stated rather than hidden.

## 4. Arguments, explanations and conditionals (slide 7)

**Explanation:** says why or how something is the case. The lecture's baby-crying example explains an accepted event.

**Argument:** offers reasons to accept a conclusion that is at issue.

**Conditional:** an if-then statement. A conditional alone does not supply the complete argument "if P then Q, P, therefore Q."

**Study clarification:** the phrase "because" can introduce either an explanation or a premise. Identify the purpose in context, rather than relying only on a keyword. The lecture's nationality/language conditional illustrates grammatical form, not a guarantee that everyone from a country speaks its language.

## 5. Breaking down an argument (slide 8)

1. Identify separate propositions, even if several occur within one sentence.
2. Find what the speaker is trying to prove.
3. Mark conclusion indicators such as therefore, thus, so, which shows that and for these reasons.
4. Find supporting propositions, using clues such as because, since, for, the reason that and as shown by.
5. State any essential unstated assumptions.

Indicators are clues, not an automatic classification rule. The conclusion can occur first, last or between premises.

## 6. Front-row example (slide 8)

The lecture observes that front-row students generally earn higher grades and recommends moving forward to improve one's GPA.

| Element | Reconstruction |
|---|---|
| Explicit premise 1 | Front-row students generally earn higher grades |
| Explicit premise 2 | You want to improve your GPA |
| Conclusion | You should move to the front |
| Unstated causal assumption | Moving forward contributes to higher grades, rather than merely correlating with them |
| Unstated practical assumption | The move is feasible and a suitable way to pursue the goal |

**Study evaluation:** motivated students might select front seats. The observation alone does not isolate seating as the cause. Compare other explanations or seek a design that tests the causal claim. Do not claim that moving seats can never help.

## 7. Five evaluation criteria (slide 9)

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Clarity | Are terms and claims unambiguous? |
| Credibility | Do the premises have adequate evidence? |
| Relevance | Do the premises bear on the conclusion? |
| Completeness | Are important premises, conclusions or assumptions omitted? |
| Soundness, in the lecture's broad checklist | Are the premises true and do they support the conclusion? |

**Study clarification on logic vocabulary:** deductive validity asks whether true premises could lead to a false conclusion. Deductive soundness requires validity and true premises. The lecture uses soundness more broadly as an evaluation question. When assessing probabilistic support, explain its strength rather than claiming certainty.

## 8. Constructing an argument (slide 10)

The lecture's eight steps are:

1. State the issue clearly as a question.
2. Develop premises that address it.
3. Eliminate weak or irrelevant premises.
4. Establish a conclusion.
5. Organize the argument.
6. Try it on other people.
7. Revise it.
8. Act on the conclusion if necessary.

**Study example:** should a study room open later during exams? Use observed unmet demand, a stated value about access, staffing costs and feasible alternatives. Consider objections before deciding. A proposed answer should identify what evidence still needs collecting rather than invent demand statistics.

## Exam focus

- Identify an issue, proposition, premise and conclusion.
- Classify the four premise types without confusing classification with truth.
- Distinguish an argument from an explanation and a conditional.
- Reconstruct the front-row argument and its hidden causal premise.
- Apply all five evaluation criteria.
- Reproduce the eight construction steps and explain how objections can improve an argument.
