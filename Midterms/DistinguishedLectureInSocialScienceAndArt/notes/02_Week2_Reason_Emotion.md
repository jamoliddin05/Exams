# Week 2: Reason and Emotion

What this lecture is about: evidence-based reasoning, constructive and harmful roles of emotion, and questions about AI, faith and reason.

Source: `DLSSA 2.pptx`, 18 slides, Chapter 2. Several slides contain discussion prompts or external links rather than full arguments. All examples below marked study examples are newly written.

## 1. The opening ethical problem (slides 2-3)

The lecture uses Raskolnikov's proposed redistribution of a victim's money in *Crime and Punishment* to ask whether a calculation of the greatest good is enough for a good decision.

**Study analysis:** examine the premises, uncertainty about consequences, harm, rights and alternatives. Calling a decision rational does not establish that its assumptions are true or its action ethically justified. The deck asks a question and does not supply a worked ethical proof.

## 2. Reason and its forms (slides 4-5)

**Reason:** supporting a claim or conclusion on the basis of evidence.

The lecture includes abstract thinking, logical argumentation and a behavioral component. It names deduction, generalization, imagination and spatial-temporal problem solving. The Temple Grandin example illustrates that reasoning is not confined to one verbal or abstract style.

Traditional views summarized on slide 5:

| View | Lecture summary |
|---|---|
| Plato | One rational and two nonrational parts of the soul: emotions and physical cravings |
| Christian religious view | God as perfectly rational and human rationality as a divine spark |
| Darwin | Reason evolved in adaptation and survival |
| Twentieth-century association | Reason identified with science |

These are compressed historical perspectives presented in the course. They are not a complete history or universal definitions of those traditions.

## 3. Assumptions about who can reason (slide 6)

The slide contrasts a traditional association of men with reason and women with emotion with the feminist claim that men and women share the same rational nature. It emphasizes lifelong learning and examining assumptions about race, gender and age.

**Source wording issue:** one sentence says greater emotion "does not mean that they are as capable" in reasoning. This contradicts the surrounding equality and anti-stereotyping bullets and appears to contain an omission. These notes do not endorse that sentence or silently present a guessed correction as a quotation.

**Study takeaway:** assess evidence and an individual's reasoning, rather than inferring competence from a gender, race or age stereotype.

## 4. Dreams and problem solving (slide 7)

The lecture states that reasoning can be unconscious or automatic and that dreams may help with elusive visual problems after extensive waking work. It suggests posing and writing a problem before sleep and recording dreams afterward.

Treat this as the lecture's proposed strategy. A dream can suggest an answer, but a critical thinker still checks the answer's evidence and reasoning. The slide does not show a study design or establish that every problem can be solved this way.

## 5. Emotion and emotional intelligence (slides 8-11)

The slides contrast cultural attitudes toward emotion and state that emotion can motivate better decisions.

**Emotional intelligence:** ability to perceive accurately, appraise and express emotion.

**Empathy:** capacity to enter into and understand others' emotions. The lecture places sympathy in parentheses alongside it. As a study clarification, feeling concern for someone and understanding their perspective need not be identical experiences.

| Role | Study example | Critical-thinking response |
|---|---|---|
| Motivation | Concern about unfair grading prompts investigation | Gather records and compare the stated criteria |
| Perspective taking | Understanding a teammate's anxiety explains an objection | Listen before judging their proposal |
| Distortion | Anger leads someone to reject a sound objection | Pause and evaluate the premise separately |
| Overconfidence | Excitement about a product obscures its limitations | Seek counterevidence before deciding |

Slide 11 names negative effects and integration of reason/emotion without elaboration. The table expands those headings with study examples.

**Study method:** name the feeling, identify the claim it accompanies, check the evidence and consider alternatives. Emotion may signal a concern without proving a factual conclusion.

## 6. AI, reason and emotion (slides 12-14)

The slides use HAL and questions about human capabilities to ask:

- Can computers think?
- Can computers feel emotion?
- Which human tasks might AI perform?
- How could AI improve or weaken our critical thinking and decision making?

The **Turing test is named**, but the slide provides no procedure, scoring rule or claim that it proves consciousness. Keep task performance, reasoning and subjective feeling separate when answering.

**Study example:** an AI assistant can suggest alternative explanations. The user should still inspect evidence and the argument. Producing a reassuring sentence alone does not establish that its author experiences empathy.

The deck poses open questions. It does not establish a current boundary of AI capability or a forecast of when AI will become conscious.

## 7. Faith-related vocabulary (slides 15-17)

| Term | Meaning used in these slides |
|---|---|
| Faith | Belief, trust and obedience to a religious deity |
| Fideism | Divine revelation through faith does not require reason |
| Rationalism in this discussion | Religion should be consistent with reason and evidence |
| Atheist | A person who does not believe in the existence of a personal God |
| Agnostic | A person who believes God's existence is ultimately unknowable |
| Critical rationalism in this discussion | Faith based on direct revelation should contain no logical inconsistency with reason |

**Terminology boundary:** these are the course's faith-related uses. Do not substitute the different use of rationalism in week 3's theory of knowledge. The definition of critical rationalism here is also context-specific and should not be treated as a general survey of philosophical usage.

Questions to examine include whether faith can be achieved through reason, whether faith without reason is desirable, and how conflicting claims could be evaluated. A useful answer states its premises and considers an objection instead of assuming all readers share the same faith commitments.

## 8. External reading task (slide 18)

The lecture links contrasting readings about AI, humanity and faith and asks for evaluation of their arguments. The decks do not contain those readings. These notes therefore provide an analysis method, not invented summaries of either author's position:

1. State each author's conclusion accurately.
2. Identify evidence and assumptions.
3. Compare their treatment of intelligence, consciousness and faith.
4. Explain which reasoning is stronger and why.
5. State what the available evidence cannot settle.
