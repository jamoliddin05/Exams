# Informal Fallacies: Reference and Repairs

Source: `DLSSA 4.pptx`, slides 6-17. The lecture groups **19 named fallacies** into 4 ambiguity, 8 relevance and 7 unwarranted-assumption fallacies. This reference preserves that taxonomy. All examples and repairs below are new study examples.

**Notes:** [Week 4 explanation](notes/04_Week4_InformalFallacies.md).

## 1. Ambiguity (W4 slides 6-8)

| Name | Faulty mechanism | New study example | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equivocation | A key word changes meaning | This bag is light. Light cannot be dark. Therefore this bag cannot be dark | Keep light meaning low in weight separate from light meaning illumination |
| Amphiboly | Grammar permits competing readings | The librarian said on Monday she would open late, so Monday must be the late-opening day | Clarify whether Monday describes the announcement or the opening |
| Accent | Emphasis or selective presentation changes meaning | A notice says free entry for members. An ad highlights free entry and implies everyone enters free | Restore the condition for members |
| Division | Group property is transferred to every member | This team is excellent, so every member must be excellent at every task | Assess the member and task separately |

The last two descriptions follow the lecture's scope and category placement. Do not replace them with another textbook's classification during course revision.

## 2. Relevance (W4 slides 9-13)

| Name | Faulty mechanism | New study example | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ad hominem | Person attacked instead of their argument | His evidence is wrong because he is rude | Evaluate the evidence and inference |
| Appeal to force | Threat used as proof | Accept my conclusion or I will remove you from the team | Supply relevant reasons without coercion |
| Appeal to pity | Irrelevant distress used as support | I had a bad day, so my factual answer must be correct | Separate compassion from factual evaluation |
| Popular appeal | Popularity substitutes for evidence | Everyone reposted it, so it is true | Check the claim's original support |
| Appeal to ignorance | No disproof becomes proof, or no proof becomes disproof | Nobody proved the rumor false, so it is true | Identify positive evidence and acknowledge uncertainty |
| Hasty generalization | Small or biased sample supports a sweeping claim | Both people I asked disliked it, so everyone dislikes it | Obtain a more suitable sample and narrow the claim |
| Straw man | Opposing view misrepresented | You want one fewer quiz, so you want students to learn nothing | Address the actual proposal |
| Red herring | Attention redirected from the issue | Asked about an incorrect bill, the shop praises its beautiful decor | Return to the billing evidence |

## 3. Unwarranted assumptions (W4 slides 14-17)

| Name | Faulty mechanism | New study example | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Begging the question | Conclusion assumed in the support | The policy is fair because it is a fair policy | Give independent grounds for fairness |
| Inappropriate appeal to authority | Irrelevant authority treated as decisive | A famous athlete says the software is secure, so it must be secure | Seek relevant expertise and evidence |
| Loaded question | Unproved premise built into a question | Why are you still hiding the results? | Establish whether results were hidden before asking why |
| False dilemma | Alternatives unjustifiably restricted | Either agree with every detail or oppose the whole project | Identify intermediate or different options |
| Questionable cause, including post hoc | Causation inferred without adequate support | The lucky sticker came before the win, so it caused the win | Examine a plausible mechanism, comparisons and alternatives |
| Slippery slope | Unsupported chain presented as inevitable | Permit one exception and soon no rules will exist | Justify each link and examine limiting conditions |
| Naturalistic fallacy | Natural origin treated as goodness | This is natural, so it is automatically good | Evaluate the relevant qualities directly |

## 4. Distinguishing similar labels

| Pair | Key difference |
|---|---|
| Equivocation / amphiboly | Changing word meaning / ambiguous grammar |
| Division / hasty generalization | Group to member / insufficient sample to wider population |
| Straw man / red herring | Distort the opponent / redirect the issue |
| Begging the question / loaded question | Circular support / presumed unproved fact in a question |
| Popular appeal / inappropriate authority | Many believers / supposedly authoritative speaker |
| Questionable cause / slippery slope | Unsupported causal inference / unsupported chain of escalating consequences |
| Popular appeal / snob appeal | Mass acceptance / exclusivity or elite status |

**Snob appeal** belongs to W6.1 s5's advertising list, and is not an additional item in W4's list of 19. Euphemism, hyperbole, analogy and statistics also appear in W6.1, but their use alone does not prove fallacious reasoning. See [advertising techniques](notes/06_Week6_Marketing_Advertising.md#4-other-persuasive-techniques-slide-5).

## 5. Answer template

1. **Conclusion:** what is the speaker asking us to accept?
2. **Support:** what reason is offered?
3. **Fallacy:** which mechanism fits the failed inference?
4. **Explanation:** identify the precise shift, irrelevance or assumption.
5. **Repair:** supply what is missing or limit the claim.

Example: "Everyone shares this story, so it must be accurate."

- Conclusion: the story is accurate.
- Support: many people share it.
- Classification: popular appeal, a relevance fallacy.
- Problem: circulation does not establish accuracy.
- Repair: inspect its original evidence and independent corroboration.

## 6. Classification cautions

- A true conclusion can be supported by a bad argument.
- An emotional response can be relevant without proving a factual claim.
- An expert's relevant evidence is different from irrelevant celebrity endorsement.
- A credible chain of consequences differs from an unsupported slippery slope.
- A statement can contain multiple weaknesses. Explain each mechanism instead of listing labels without analysis.
- A short quotation may lack context. State the reading your diagnosis assumes.
