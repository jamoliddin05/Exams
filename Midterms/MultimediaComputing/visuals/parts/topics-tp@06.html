Digital Images from Zero

A digital image is a grid of numbers. This lecture follows light from the scene to that grid: what the eye can tell apart, how a lens and a sensor form the picture, how sampling and quantization make it digital, and how many bits it takes. It ends with the first operations on those numbers: resampling, point transformations and the histogram. Lecture 2 did sampling and quantization for sound; here the same two steps happen in two dimensions.

Words you need

Image f(x, y) and pixel

"An image is a 2D function f(x, y)". x, y say where; f(x, y) is the intensity (gray level) there. One grid cell is a pixel, a picture element.

k bits, L levels

Each pixel stores k bits, so it takes one of L = 2k gray levels, 0 (black) to L − 1 (white). k = 8 gives 0 to 255.

Rods and cones

The two receptors in the retina. Cones: fovea, color, good light. Rods: no color, dim light.

Weber ratio ΔIc/I

The smallest visible brightness change ΔIc divided by the background I. Small ratio = good discrimination.

Sampling

"Digitizing the coordinate values": which points (x, y) are kept. It fixes M × N.

Quantization

"Digitizing the amplitude values": each value is rounded to one of L levels. It fixes k.

Spatial resolution

"Smallest discernible detail", in line pairs or dots (pixels) per unit distance, for example dpi.

Intensity resolution

"Smallest discernible change in intensity level", given as the number of levels L.

Interpolation

Estimating a value between known pixels. Needed whenever an image is resampled.

Point operation, LUT

Each new pixel depends only on the same old pixel, through a fixed table of 256 entries, the look-up table (LUT).

1. The eye: two receptors, two kinds of vision (slides 3–5)

"Human visual perception plays a key role in selecting a technique" (slide 4), so the lecture starts with the eye. The cornea and lens focus light on the retina, which has two receptors.

Wherein the fovea, the center of the retinaspread over the retina, "a general overall picture of the view"
Respond tohighly sensitive to colorinsensitive to color, sensitive to low levels of illumination
Visionphotopic, good light: colorscotopic, low light: black and white
  • Slide 5: "Scotopic vision uses only rods to see, meaning that objects are visible, but appear in black and white, whereas photopic vision uses cones and provides color."
  • Brightness adaptation (Fig. 2.4). The eye works from the scotopic threshold up to the glare limit, but "the total range of distinct intensity levels the eye can discriminate simultaneously is rather small". It adapts to one brightness adaptation level Ba at a time.

2. The Weber ratio: how small a change can you see? (slides 6–7)

  • Experiment (slide 6): a background of intensity I, with a circle flashed at I + ΔI. "ΔI starts at zero and is increased slowly" until the observer sees the circle.
  • That ΔI is the just noticeable difference ΔIc. "The ratio ∆I/I is called the Weber ratio."

Weber ratio = ΔIc / I small → good brightness discrimination · large → poor

background I = 100
circle seen at ΔIc = 2:    2 / 100 = 0.02   a 2% change is visible    → good
circle seen at ΔIc = 20:  20 / 100 = 0.2    a 20% change is needed    → poor
ΔIc goes on top. 100 / 2 = 50 is the ratio upside down.

3. Seeing is not measuring (slides 8–11)

  • "Perceived intensity is not a simple function of the actual intensity" (slide 8).
  • Mach band effect (Fig. 2.7): the actual intensity is a staircase of flat bands. The perceived intensity undershoots and overshoots at each boundary, so edges look sharper than they are.
  • Simultaneous contrast (Fig. 2.8, slide 9): all the inner squares have the same intensity. They appear progressively darker as the background becomes lighter.
  • Object perception (slides 10–11) works in levels: features, then groups of features, then objects, then scenes.
  • Bottom-up processing is data-driven: the sensation reaches the brain, then the brain makes sense of it. Top-down processing: what we know informs the sensation, as when you walk to the refrigerator in the middle of the night.

4. How a lens forms the image (slides 12–16)

  • Acquisition records geometry (size, location) and appearance (color, intensity) (slide 12).
  • The image on the retina or the image plane is upside down (Fig. 2.3, slide 13). Similar triangles give its size.
15 m tree, 100 m away, 17 mm from the lens center C to the retina
15 / 100 = h / 17   →   h = 17 × 15 / 100 = 2.55 mm      (textbook context)

thin lens 1/S₁ + 1/S₂ = 1/f field of view ω = 2 arctan(d / f)

f = 50 mm, object at S₁ = 2000 mm:   1/S₂ = 1/50 − 1/2000 = 0.0195   →   S₂ = 51.28 mm
d = 18 mm (half of a 36 mm frame, textbook context):
  f = 28 → ω = 65.5°    f = 50 → 39.6°    f = 70 → 28.8°    f = 210 → 9.8°
  • S₁ is the object distance, S₂ the image distance, f the focal length, d the half-size measured from the optical axis.
  • Slide 14: "Increasing the distance from the object to the lens will reduce the size of image". "Large focus length will give a small FOV."
  • Depth of field (DOF, slide 15) is proportional to S₁ and inversely proportional to f. "The longer the focal length, the narrower the angle of view and the higher the magnification." Slide 16 shows it with 28, 50, 70 and 210 mm lenses.

5. Light and the EM spectrum (slides 17–19)

  • The sun releases energy in "rays" with properties of particles and of waves: electromagnetic radiation.
  • By wavelength (slide 17): radio 10³ m, microwave 10⁻² m, infrared 10⁻⁵ m, visible 10⁻⁶ m, ultraviolet 10⁻⁸ m, X-ray 10⁻¹⁰ m, gamma 10⁻¹² m. Frequencies run from 10⁴ to 10²⁰ Hz.

λ = c / ν, c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s E = h ν, h = 6.626068 × 10⁻³⁴ m² kg/s

ν = 5 × 10¹⁴ Hz:  λ = 2.998 × 10⁸ / 5 × 10¹⁴ = 5.996 × 10⁻⁷ m = 599.6 nm   (visible)
                  E = 6.626068 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5 × 10¹⁴ = 3.313 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
higher frequency → shorter wavelength → more energy
  • Diffraction limit (slide 19): the Airy disk is "the best-focused spot of light that a perfect lens with a circular aperture can make". Its size ∼ λ · f/d (f/d is the f-number). Smaller detail cannot be seen.

6. From light to numbers: f(x, y) and f = i · r (slides 20–23)

  • Sensing (slide 20): incoming energy becomes a voltage, which is digitized. Sensors: single, line or array (Fig. 2.12).
  • A digital image has x, y and f(x, y) all finite, for example x ∈ {1, …, M}, y ∈ {1, …, N}, f ∈ {0, …, 255} (slide 21).
  • Spatial domain model (slide 22): g(x, y) = T[f(x, y)]. T works on a neighborhood of (x, y), for example 3 × 3. With a 1 × 1 neighborhood T is a point operation (sections 11–12).

f(x, y) = i(x, y) · r(x, y) 0 < i < ∞ illumination (source) · 0 < r < 1 reflectance (object)

  • i: sunlight 10,000 lm/m² (cloudy), 90,000 lm/m² (clear day); office 1000 lm/m². r: black velvet 0.01, white pall 0.8, snow 0.93.
  • Since r < 1, always f < i. In practice Lmin < f < Lmax with Lmin = iminrmin, Lmax = imaxrmax.

7. Sampling, quantization and dynamic range (slides 24–26)

  • "Sampling = digitizing the coordinate values". The sensor usually decides it (slide 25: the image is projected onto a CCD array).
  • "Quantization = digitizing the amplitude values". Each sample is rounded to the nearest of L levels.

0 ≤ f(x, y) ≤ L − 1, L = 2^k (k bits per pixel)

  • Dynamic range (contrast ratio, slide 26): "the ratio of the maximum detectable intensity level (saturation) to the minimum detectable intensity level (noise)".

DR = I_max / I_min (saturation / noise)

sensor: saturation 60,000, noise 20   →   DR = 60,000 / 20 = 3,000
with the values of Figure 2:

  cloudy day, all three      100 to 9,300       inside          → recorded

9. Spatial and intensity resolution (slides 29–31)

  • Spatial resolution = "smallest discernible detail", in line pairs or dots (pixels) per unit distance. Print uses dpi: newspaper, then magazines, then books. Fig. 2.20 shows one image at 1250, 300, 150 and 72 dpi.
  • "Large image size itself does not mean high spatial resolution!" What counts is how many pixels the object covers.
  • Intensity resolution = "smallest discernible change in intensity level", the number of levels L.
  • Too few levels turn smooth shading into flat bands with false edges: false contouring (the skull at 256 down to 2 levels). The slide says undersampling; strictly it is coarse quantization (textbook context).
  • Isopreference curves (Fig. 2.22–2.23, slide 31): N and k are varied together, and each curve joins the (N, k) pairs of equal subjective quality. Test images: Face (low detail), Cameraman (medium), Crowd (high).
  • The Crowd curve is nearly vertical: a detailed image needs fewer intensity levels for the same quality (textbook context).

10. Sets, neighbours and adjacency (slides 32–35)

  • Set notation (slide 32): a ∈ A, a ∉ A, subset A ⊆ B, union A ∪ B, intersection A ∩ B, disjoint when A ∩ B = ∅, difference A − B = {w | w ∈ A, w ∉ B}, and the set universe of all elements.
  • "A region in an image is represented by a set of coordinates within the region" (slide 33). Union, intersection, complement and difference then work on regions. Lecture 10 builds morphology on this.
p = (x, y), x = row:
N₄(p) = (x−1, y) (x+1, y) (x, y−1) (x, y+1)                up, down, left, right
N_D(p) = (x−1, y−1) (x−1, y+1) (x+1, y−1) (x+1, y+1)      the 4 diagonals
N₈(p) = N₄(p) ∪ N_D(p)
p = (2, 3):  N₄ = (1,3) (3,3) (2,2) (2,4)    N_D = (1,2) (1,4) (3,2) (3,4)
slide 34 prints the bottom-left diagonal as (x−1, y+1); it is (x+1, y−1)
  • Adjacency (slide 35) needs a set V of values that count, V = {1} in a binary image. Both f(p) and f(q) must be in V.
  • 4-adjacent: q ∈ N₄(p). 8-adjacent: q ∈ N₈(p). m-adjacent: q ∈ N₄(p), or q ∈ N_D(p) and N₄(p) ∩ N₄(q) holds no pixel with a value in V.
  • Slide example, rows 011 / 010 / 001, p = (1, 1): q = (0, 2) is 8-adjacent but not m-adjacent, because the shared 4-neighbour (0, 1) is 1. q = (2, 2) is m-adjacent, because (1, 2) and (2, 1) are 0. m-adjacency removes the double path that 8-adjacency creates. Lecture 7 covers this in full.

11. Resampling and interpolation (slide 36)

"Need to resample the image when": rescaling; geometrical transformation; "the output image coordinates are not discrete". In each case a new pixel lands between old pixel centers, and its value is estimated from the pixels around it.

Linear interpolation in 1D: a point a of the way from v₀ to v₁ gets v₀ + a(v₁ − v₀). Bilinear does it along both rows, then once between them. The slide gives no formula; this is textbook context.

f = (1−a)(1−b) f₀₀ + a(1−b) f₁₀ + (1−a)b f₀₁ + ab f₁₁ a = offset across, b = offset down

f₀₀ = 10   f₁₀ = 20   (top row)          a = 0.3, b = 0.6
f₀₁ = 30   f₁₁ = 60   (bottom row)

bottom row  30 + 0.3 × (60 − 30) = 39
between     13 + 0.6 × (39 − 13) = 28.6

nearest     a = 0.3 → left, b = 0.6 → bottom   → f₀₁ = 30

12. Point operations: stretching, threshold, negative, gamma (slides 37–57)

"Intensity transformation is a point process that converts an old pixel into a new pixel based on some predefined function", "implemented with the LUT" (slides 45, 49). The function is computed once for the 256 inputs, then each pixel is a table look-up.

contrast stretching: 0 for x ≤ low · 255 (x − low)/(high − low) between · 255 for x ≥ high

low = 50, high = 150:   x = 30 → 0    x = 80 → 255 × 30/100 = 76.5    x = 100 → 127.5    x = 200 → 255
general form (slide 38), B bits:  y = (2^B − 1) / (x_max − x_min) · (x − x_min)
negative (slide 45):  y = −x + 255     100 → 155
  • Slide 39: basic stretching vs end-in search, which lets a small share of pixels clip at each end (textbook context). Slide 40: the stretched histogram has gaps (a comb); no new levels appear. Slide 41: color images stretch R, G and B separately.
  • Threshold (slides 42–43): 0 below m, 255 above. A lower m makes more pixels white (m = 128 and 100 on the slides).
  • Negative: applications are display of medical images and producing negative prints. Intensity level slicing highlights a range [a, b], for segmentation of certain gray-level regions (slide 44).
  • Posterizing reduces the number of gray levels; thresholding is posterizing to 2 levels. A bounded ("bounced") threshold thresholds only a limited range and leaves the rest as the null transform (slide 46).
  • Gamma correction "is important for displaying images on a screen correctly, to prevent bleaching or darkening of images when viewed from different types of monitors". "Our eyes perceive images in a gamma-shaped curve, whereas cameras capture images in a linear fashion" (slide 47).
  • Slide 47 code: np.array(255*(img/255)**gamma, dtype='uint8'). uint8 truncates the fraction.
  • Two conventions. Slides 47–48 use s = 255(r/255)γ: γ < 1 brightens (0.45 brighter, 2.20 darker). Slide 49 writes output = input1/γ and slide 53 follows it, so there 0.45 darkens. Write the formula you use.
r = 64, s = 255 (r/255)^γ
γ = 0.5:  255 × (64/255)^0.5 = 127.75   → uint8 127    brighter
γ = 2.2:  255 × (64/255)^2.2 = 12.18    → 12           darker
log transform (textbook context, not on the slides): s = c log(1 + r), c = 255 / log 256
  r = 3 → 63.75     r = 15 → 127.5     (dark values lifted; the log base does not matter)

  • Slide 59: a dark image has a narrow histogram on the left, a washed-out image a narrow one on the right, a high-contrast image a spread one.
  • Slide 60: +40 shifts the histogram right (spike at 255 from clipping), −40 shifts it left (spike at 0). Slide 61: ×1.2 stretches it (gaps), /1.2 compresses it.
  • Slides 62–63: an RGB image has 3 histograms, a gray image 1.
  • Histogram equalization (slide 64): the goal is "a uniform histogram for the output image". Steps: compute the histogram; calculate the normalized sum of the histogram; transform the input image to the output image. Lecture 9 does it with numbers.

Mistakes that cost marks

  • Multiplying by L instead of k. 64 levels means k = log₂ 64 = 6, so 512 × 512 × 6 bits. Divide by 8 for bytes.
  • Inverting the Weber ratio. It is ΔIc/I, and small is good.
  • Swapping rods and cones. Scotopic (dim) = rods, black and white. Photopic (bright) = cones, color, fovea.
  • Swapping sampling and quantization. Sampling → coordinates, M × N, spatial resolution. Quantization → amplitude, k, intensity resolution, false contouring.
  • "More pixels = more detail." Large image size itself does not mean high spatial resolution.
  • Bilinear: a goes with the column, b with the row; check the four weights sum to 1. Nearest neighbour rounds each offset.
  • Gamma without the convention. With s = 255(r/255)γ, γ < 1 brightens; slide 49's input1/γ reverses it. The uint8 code truncates (127.75 → 127).
  • A diagonal neighbour is m-adjacent only if the shared 4-neighbours hold no value in V. N_D's bottom-left is (x+1, y−1).

Cheat sheet

How to write it on the exam

IMAGE REPRESENTATION
  1. Sampling = digitizing the coordinate values; quantization = digitizing the amplitude values.
  2. Spatial resolution = smallest discernible detail (dpi);
     intensity resolution = smallest discernible change in intensity level (L levels).
  3. k = log₂ L (round up); gray values 0 … L − 1.
  4. b = M × N × k bits, written out; ÷ 8 = bytes.
  5. Fewer levels: new k and b; artifact = false contouring, spatial resolution unchanged.

PERCEPTION AND IMAGE FORMATION
  1. Rods vs cones table: where, respond to, scotopic / photopic.
  2. Weber ratio = ΔIc / I per case; smaller = better; dim light → rods.
  3. f = i · r in a table; the ratio between objects = ratio of r (i cancels); f < i.
  4. DR = I_max / I_min = saturation / noise; check brightest < saturation, darkest > noise.

RESAMPLING AND INTERPOLATION
  1. When: rescaling; geometrical transformation; output coordinates not discrete.
  2. NN 1 pixel (fast, simple, loss of sharpness, checkerboard) · bilinear 4 · bicubic 16 (sharpest, slow).
  3. Offsets a (column), b (row); two row interpolations, one between; weight check.
  4. NN: round the coordinates, read that pixel.  Resized storage: halving both sides → 1/4.

INTENSITY TRANSFORMATIONS
  1. Definition: point process, old pixel → new pixel by a predefined function, LUT.
  2. Negative s = 255 − r; medical images, negative prints.
  3. Gamma: state s = 255 (r/255)^γ, compute each value; γ < 1 brightens.
  4. Stretching: 0 below low, 255 above high, 255(x − low)/(high − low) between.
  5. Say whether you round or truncate.
Exam focus
  • Cones: fovea, color, photopic. Rods: no color, low light, scotopic (black and white).
  • Weber ratio ΔIc/I: 2/100 = 0.02 good, 20/100 = 0.2 poor. The rod branch is high, the cone branch falls to about 10−1.6 ≈ 0.025.
  • f = i · r with 0 < r < 1: office × snow = 1000 × 0.93 = 930. Changing i scales every value; object ratios stay.
  • Sampling = coordinates, quantization = amplitude. L = 2k, 0 ≤ f ≤ L − 1. DR = saturation / noise.
  • b = M × N × k: 1024² × 8 = 8,388,608 bits = 1 MiB. Table 2.1 error: 2048² × 7 = 29,360,128.
  • Too few levels → false contouring. Large image size does not mean high spatial resolution.
  • Resample when rescaling, after a geometrical transformation, or when output coordinates are not discrete. NN 1 pixel, bilinear 4, bicubic 16 (sharpest, slow).
  • Bilinear 10, 20, 30, 60 at a = 0.3, b = 0.6 → 13, 39, 28.6; nearest neighbour 30.
  • Stretch 50–150: 80 → 76.5. Negative 255 − r. Gamma r = 64: γ = 0.5 → 127, γ = 2.2 → 12.
  • Lens: 1/S₁ + 1/S₂ = 1/f, ω = 2 arctan(d/f), longer f → smaller FOV. λ = c/ν, E = hν.
