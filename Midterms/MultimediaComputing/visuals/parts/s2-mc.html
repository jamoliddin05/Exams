Question 1 · Lecture 2, Sampling theorem and aliasing

An analog audio signal contains frequencies up to 12 kHz. Which sampling rate will cause aliasing?

  1. a 48 kHz No. 48 > 24 kHz, so every frequency up to 12 kHz is captured (4 samples per cycle).
  2. b 30 kHz No. 30 > 24 kHz, 2.5 samples per cycle of the highest frequency.
  3. c 25 kHz No. 25 > 24 kHz. It is only just above the limit, and that is enough.
  4. d 20 kHz Correct. 20 < 24 kHz, so the 12 kHz content comes back as a false 8 kHz tone.

Answer: d. The sampling theorem says to sample at more than twice the highest frequency: fs > 2 × 12 = 24 kHz. Only 20 kHz is below that limit.

A 20 kHz sampler can only represent frequencies below fs/2 = 10 kHz. The 12 kHz tone is undersampled and is played back as a tone at 20 − 12 = 8 kHz. This loss of information during digitization is aliasing.

Exam line

Sample at a frequency greater than twice the maximal frequency in the signal, fs > 2fmax. Sampling below that undersamples, and "aliasing is the term used to describe loss of information during digitization."

Question 2 · Lecture 3, Structure of MIDI messages

How many MIDI channels are there, and where is the channel number stored in a channel message?

  1. a 16 channels (0 to 15), in the four low-order bits of the status byte Correct. Four bits give 2⁴ = 16 values, 0000 to 1111.
  2. b 16 channels (0 to 15), in the four most significant bits of the status byte No. The top bit is the status flag 1, and the next three bits hold the message.
  3. c 8 channels, in the three message bits of the status byte No. The three middle bits hold the message type (note on, note off, ...).
  4. d 128 channels, in the first data byte No. A data byte has 7 free bits, 0 to 127, for values such as the key number.

Answer: a. MIDI has 16 channels, numbered 0 to 15. A channel message starts with a status byte whose most significant bit is 1. The four low-order bits identify the channel and the three remaining bits hold the message, so the byte reads 1ttt nnnn.

Exam line

16 MIDI channels, numbered from 0 to 15. Status byte 1ttt nnnn: most significant bit set to 1, the four low-order bits identify the channel, the three remaining bits hold the message. Data byte 0ddddddd.

Question 3 · Lecture 4, Entropy

A source emits 8 different symbols. Its entropy is largest when ... , and the largest value is ...

  1. a one symbol has probability close to 1; 3 bits/symbol No. A near-certain symbol carries almost no information, so the entropy is close to 0.
  2. b all symbols are equally likely; 8 bits/symbol No. 8 is the number of symbols. 8 bits/symbol needs 256 equally likely symbols.
  3. c all symbols are equally likely; 3 bits/symbol Correct. log₂ 8 = 3.
  4. d all symbols are equally likely; 1 bit/symbol No. 1 bit is the maximum for 2 symbols.

Answer: c. Entropy η = −Σ pi log₂ pi is the average information per symbol. It is largest when no symbol is easier to predict than another, so all 8 have p = 1/8. Each symbol then carries log₂ 8 = 3 bits and η = 8 × (1/8) × 3 = 3 bits/symbol. The slide's example works the same way: 256 equally likely gray levels give log₂ 256 = 8 bits.

Exam line

η = Σ pi log₂(1/pi). A uniform distribution gives the maximum, log₂ N bits per symbol: 3 bits for 8 symbols, 8 bits for 256 gray levels.

Question 4 · Lecture 5, Frequency masking

Which statement about frequency masking, as used by MPEG audio (MP3), is true?

  1. a A higher tone masks a lower tone better than the reverse No. This is backwards. Masking reaches much further up in frequency than down.
  2. b A lower tone can mask a higher tone Correct. This is the first rule on slide 14.
  3. c Two tones that are widely separated in frequency mask each other strongly No. Widely separated tones give little masking.
  4. d The weaker the masking tone, the wider the range of frequencies it masks No. The greater the power of the masking tone, the wider its influence.

Answer: b. A loud tone raises the hearing threshold around its own frequency, and the raised region reaches much further above the tone than below it. So a lower tone can mask a higher tone, and the reverse is not true.

A more powerful masker widens the region, and two tones far apart barely mask each other. MPEG audio uses these rules to remove sounds that are masked anyway.

Exam line

Frequency masking: a lower tone can mask a higher tone; the reverse is not true. The greater the power of the masking tone, the wider its influence, so widely separated tones give little masking. MPEG audio removes sounds which are masked anyway.

Question 5 · Lecture 6, Image resampling and interpolation

When an image is resampled, bilinear interpolation computes each new pixel from how many nearest original pixels?

  1. a 1 No. One pixel is nearest neighbour: copy the closest original pixel.
  2. b 4 Correct. The 2×2 block around the new position.
  3. c 9 No. None of the three slide methods uses a 3×3 block. 9 pixels is the size of a 3×3 filter mask.
  4. d 16 No. 16 pixels, a 4×4 block, is bicubic.

Answer: b. Bilinear interpolation takes the 2 × 2 = 4 original pixels around the new position. It interpolates linearly along x on the two rows, then once along y between those two results. Nearest neighbour uses 1 pixel and bicubic uses 16. The slide lists the three methods; the pixel counts are textbook context.

Exam line

Resample when rescaling, applying a geometrical transformation, or when the output coordinates are not discrete. Nearest neighbor: 1 pixel, fast and simple, loss of sharpness. Bilinear: the 4 nearest pixels. Bicubic: 16 pixels, sharpest, slow.

Question 6 · Lecture 7, Connectivity and adjacency

Why is m-adjacency (mixed adjacency) used?

  1. a To eliminate the multiple path connections that arise with 8-adjacency Correct. This is the slide 24 wording.
  2. b To connect two pixels even when one of their values is not in V No. 4-, 8- and m-adjacency all need both values in V.
  3. c To make every diagonal neighbour adjacent, exactly as 8-adjacency does No. m-adjacency drops a diagonal link when a shared 4-neighbour is in V.
  4. d To measure the city-block (D4) distance between two pixels No. D₄ = |x − s| + |y − t| is a distance measure and ignores V.

Answer: a. With 8-adjacency, three pixels in an L shape form a triangle, so two different paths join the same pair. m-adjacency keeps every 4-neighbour link. It keeps a diagonal link only when the two pixels share no 4-neighbour with a value in V. The redundant diagonal is dropped and one path remains.

Exam line

p and q with values from V are m-connected if q ∈ N₄(p), or q ∈ ND(p) and N₄(p) ∩ N₄(q) has no pixels whose values are from V. "m-connectivity eliminates the multiple path connections that arises in 8-connectivity."

Question 7 · Lecture 9, Lookup tables

A 3-bit image is passed through the look-up table (LUT) below. What does a pixel with value 5 become?

Index01234567
Output12334677
  1. a 4 No. That is the 5th entry counted from 1, which is index 4.
  2. b 5 No. Only the identity LUT leaves a value unchanged, and this LUT is not the identity.
  3. c 6 Correct. LUT[5] = 6.
  4. d 7 No. 7 is stored at indexes 6 and 7.

Answer: c. A LUT uses the current pixel value as the array index, and the new value is the array element at that index. Indexes start at 0, so value 5 reads the entry under index 5, which is 6. Counting entries from 1 lands on index 4 and gives the wrong answer 4.

Exam line

"An LUT is simply a table of cross-references linking index numbers to output values." Use the current pixel value as the array index; the new value is the array element pointed by this index. Indexes start at 0.

Question 8 · Lecture 10, Opening and closing

Morphological closing of a binary image with a small structuring element is:

  1. a an erosion followed by a dilation; it removes small bright spots No. Erosion first is opening, and removing small bright spots is opening's effect.
  2. b a dilation followed by an erosion; it removes thin protrusions and breaks narrow isthmuses No. The order is right, but these effects belong to opening.
  3. c an erosion followed by a dilation; it fills small holes No. Erosion first is opening, and opening does not fill holes.
  4. d a dilation followed by an erosion; it fills small holes (dark spots) and narrow gaps Correct. "Closing can remove small dark spots."

Answer: d. Closing is A • B = (A ⊕ B) ⊖ B. The dilation grows the object and swallows any hole or gap narrower than B. The erosion then shrinks the outline back to its old place. A filled hole has no background pixel left inside, so the erosion cannot reopen it.

Opening is the other order, erosion then dilation. It removes small bright spots and thin protrusions and breaks narrow isthmuses, which is what options a and b describe.

Exam line

Closing is a dilation followed by an erosion, A • B = (A ⊕ B) ⊖ B. "Closing can remove small dark spots and connect small bright cracks." Opening is an erosion followed by a dilation and can remove small bright spots.

Question 9 · Lecture 11, Median filtering

Which filter is best for removing salt-and-pepper (impulse) noise while preserving edges?

  1. a 3x3 average (low-pass) filter No. It smears each spike into its neighbours and blurs the edge. Low-pass suits Gaussian noise.
  2. b Median filter Correct. "An effective method for removing impulse noise" that keeps sharp edges.
  3. c Max filter No. Max removes only dark spikes, and it grows every bright spike.
  4. d Laplacian filter No. The Laplacian is an edge operator. It makes the spikes stand out even more.

Answer: b. The median filter sorts the window and keeps the middle value. A lone spike always sorts to one end of the list, so it never becomes the output. At an edge the side with more pixels in the window wins, so the edge stays sharp.

The average mixes the spike into every output it touches. Max and min each remove one kind of spike, and both fail on mixed impulse noise.

Exam line

Median filtering is "an effective method for removing impulse noise" and preserves sharp edges; a lowpass filter is suited for removing Gaussian noise. Min removes white spikes, max removes dark spikes, and both fail on mixed impulse noise.

Question 10 · Lecture 12, Statistical pattern recognition

In a statistical pattern recognition system, the "reject class" is:

  1. a the class with the largest number of training samples No. Class size plays no part in rejection.
  2. b the set of pixels removed during segmentation No. Segmentation separates objects from the background before any feature is measured.
  3. c a generic class for objects that are not in any of the designated known classes Correct. This is the slide 36 definition.
  4. d the cluster that unsupervised learning forms first No. Clusters come from unsupervised learning. Reject is an output of the classifier.

Answer: c. A classifier takes the feature vector and assigns it to one of the designated classes or to the reject class. Reject is the generic class for objects that match none of the known classes. With the nearest class mean, the object goes to the closest class only if it is close enough, and it is rejected otherwise.

Exam line

Reject class: "a generic class for objects not in any of the designated known classes". A classifier inputs the feature vector and assigns it to one of a set of designated classes or to the reject class.
