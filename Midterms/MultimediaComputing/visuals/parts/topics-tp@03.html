Lecture 3 · Week 2 · 7 sample questions

SNR, MIDI and PCM from Zero

Lecture 2 turned sound into numbers by sampling and quantization. This lecture measures how good those numbers are (the signal-to-noise ratio in decibels, and the quantization noise that each extra bit halves) and what they cost in bits per second. It then shows how MIDI sends music as tiny event messages instead of samples, and how PCM codes speech for transmission.

Words you need

Decibel (dB)

A tenth of a bel. It turns a ratio into a small number: a power ratio R is 10 log₁₀ R dB. Ten times the power is +10 dB.

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)

"The ratio of the power of the correct signal to the noise", in dB. Larger means a cleaner signal.

Quantization noise

The roundoff error from storing only a fixed set of levels. At most half of one interval.

SQNR

Signal-to-quantization-noise ratio: the largest signal against the largest quantization error, in dB. About 6.02 dB per bit.

Bits per sample N

How many bits store each sample, "typically 8 or 16". N bits give 2N levels.

Bitrate

Bits sent per second: sampling rate × bits per sample × channels. kbps is kilobits per second, kB/s is kilobytes per second.

MIDI

Musical Instrument Digital Interface, a protocol from the early 1980s. It sends music as events (which note, how loud, which instrument) instead of sound samples.

Status byte, data byte

A MIDI message starts with a status byte (128 to 255, top bit 1) that says what to do. Data bytes (0 to 127, top bit 0) carry the values.

MIDI channel

One of 16 lanes, numbered 0 to 15, usually one instrument each. Its number sits in the low four bits of the status byte.

PCM

Pulse Code Modulation. Filter the signal, quantize its samples, and send each sample as a binary codeword.

1. Aliasing: a camera that samples too slowly (slide 3)

Sampling means measuring a signal at regular instants and keeping only those numbers (Lecture 2). Slide 3 opens this lecture with what goes wrong when the instants are too far apart.

  • "Examples of temporal aliasing can be seen in western movies, by observing the motion of stagecoach wheels." As the coach starts, the wheels turn forward. As it speeds up, "the wheels appear to rotate in the opposite direction".
  • The slide's reason: "the movie camera has under sampled the wheels' motion". Each film frame is one sample of where the spokes are (a film camera takes 24 frames per second, textbook context).
  • All spokes look alike, so the eye matches each spoke to the nearest spoke position in the next frame. If the wheel turns more than half the gap between two spokes per frame, the nearest match lies behind it, and the wheel seems to turn backwards.
  • The same rule for sound is the Nyquist rule of Lecture 2 (textbook context here). Take at least two samples per cycle of the fastest component. A tone above half the sampling rate is heard at a false, alias frequency.

fₛ ≥ 2 f_max · (sampling rate at least twice the highest frequency)

2. Signal-to-noise ratio and decibels (slides 4–6)

Every analog signal carries some noise. The SNR says how much stronger the signal is than that noise.

  • Slide 4: "In any analog system, random fluctuations produce noise added to the signal, and the measured voltage is thus incorrect. The ratio of the power of the correct signal to the noise is called the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)." It is a measure of the quality of the signal.
  • Power ratios in sound span huge ranges (Table 1 below covers a factor of 10¹⁶). A logarithm turns them into small numbers. The SNR "is usually measured in decibels (dB), where 1 dB is a tenth of a bel".
  • A power ratio R is 10 log₁₀ R dB. Slide 6 adds the key fact: "The power in a signal is proportional to the square of the voltage."
  • So a voltage ratio is squared first, and a square inside a logarithm is a factor 2 outside it. That is where the 20 comes from.

SNR = 10 log₁₀ (V²signal / V²noise) = 20 log₁₀ (Vsignal / Vnoise) dB

slide 6: Vsignal = 10 × Vnoise  → 20 log₁₀ 10   = 20 × 1 = 20 dB

The rule: 10 log for power ratios, 20 log for voltage (amplitude) ratios. A voltage ratio of 10 and a power ratio of 100 describe the same signal, and both give 20 dB.

  • Slide 5, Table 1, lists the "magnitudes of common sounds, in decibels". Here the decibel compares a sound with a fixed reference: "a just-audible sound with frequency 1 kHz", the quietest sound we can hear, which is 0 dB (slide 6).
  • Reading it: +10 dB is 10 times the intensity (power). +20 dB is 10 times the amplitude (voltage, pressure).

3. Quantization noise and SQNR (slides 7–9)

A digital signal stores only quantized values. So even a perfectly clean input picks up an error when it is digitized.

  • "The precision of each sample is determined by the number of bits per sample, typically 8 or 16." N bits give 2N different values.
  • Slide 7: with voltages from 0 to 1 and only 8 bits, "we effectively force all continuous values of voltage into only 256 different values". This roundoff error "is not really noise", but it is called quantization noise (or quantization error), because it occurs essentially randomly from sample to sample.
  • Slide 8: "Quantization noise is defined as the difference between the value of the analog signal, for the particular sampling time, and the nearest quantization interval value. At most, this error can be as much as half of the interval."
  • The interval (step) is the range divided by the number of levels (textbook context): 1 V / 256 = 0.0039 V, so the error is at most 0.00195 V.

step δ = range / 2N · largest error = δ / 2

  • Slide 8: "The SQNR reflects the relationship between the maximum nominal signal strength and the quantization error (also known as quantization noise) introduced in the analog-to-digital conversion."
  • Slide 9 measures both in steps. The peak signal is 2N−1 steps, because signed N-bit samples run from −2N−1 to 2N−1 − 1 (textbook context). The worst error is half a step, so the ratio is 2N−1 / ½ = 2N.
  • Both are voltages, so the factor is 20. "log 2" on the slide is log₁₀ 2 = 0.30103, and 20 × 0.30103 = 6.02.

SQNR = 20 log₁₀ (Vsignal / Vquan noise) = 20 log₁₀ (2N−1 / ½) = 20 × N × log 2 = 6.02N dB

  • Slide 9: "each bit adds about 6 dB of resolution, so 16 bits provide a maximum SQNR of 96 dB."
  • A more realistic version is SQNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB. It assumes "that the input signal is sinusoidal, that quantization error is statistically independent, and that its magnitude is uniformly distributed between 0 and half the interval". "Since larger is better, this shows that a more realistic approximation gives a better characterization number for the quality of a system."

4. Audio filtering (slide 10)

  • "The audio signal is filtered to remove unwanted frequencies. The frequencies kept depend on the application."
  • Speech: "typically from 50 Hz to 10 kHz is retained". Music: "from about 20 Hz up to 20 kHz".
  • The filter is "a bandpass filter, also called a band-limiting filter, which screens out lower and higher frequencies". It keeps one band between a low and a high cutoff.
  • "At the DA converter end, even though we have removed high frequencies that are likely just noise in any event, they reappear in the output." The reason is sampling and then quantization.
  • "Therefore, at the decoder side, a low-pass filter is used after the DA circuit, making use of the same cutoff as at the high-frequency end of the coder's bandpass filter." A low-pass filter keeps everything below one cutoff.

6. Synthetic sounds: FM and wave table (slides 13–14)

  • "Digitized sound must still be converted to analog, for us to hear it." There are "two fundamentally different approaches": FM, for frequency modulation, and Wave Table, or just Wave, sound.
  • FM "refers to the means of encoding the audio signal on the carrier frequency". The carrier is a fast cosine. The modulating signal goes inside its argument and pushes its phase back and forth. FM radio stations "operate in the 88 – 108 MHz band".
  • Wave table stores digital samples of real instruments. Its advantage is that it "much more precisely stores the exact sound of an instrument" (slide 27).

7. MIDI: music as events (slides 15–19)

  • Slide 15: "MIDI, which dates from the early 1980s, is an acronym that stands for Musical Instrument Digital Interface. It forms a protocol adopted by the electronic music industry that enables computers, synthesizers, keyboards, and other musical devices to communicate with each other."
  • Most synthesizers support it, so a sound made on one can be played on another. A computer needs a MIDI interface, built into most sound cards, and "the sound card must also have both DA and AD converters".
  • Slide 16: "MIDI is a scripting language". It codes "events" that stand for the production of certain sounds, for example the pitch of a single note, its volume and what instrument sound to play. "Therefore, MIDI files are generally very small."
  • Role: "MIDI makes music notes", so it is useful for "inventing, editing, and exchanging musical ideas", and "a very useful vehicle for music education".
  • Master and slave (slide 17): one MIDI instrument controls others, which play the same music in part. A keyboard with poor built-in sound can be "daisy-chained" to a synthesizer with excellent sound. "Since MIDI comes with a built-in timecode, the master's clock can be used to synchronize all the slave timecodes."
  • A "sequencer-sampler" reorders and manipulates digital-audio samples and sequences of MIDI. A Digital Audio Workstation such as ProTools records many tracks, for example 8 vocal tracks and 8 instrument tracks at once.
slide 21: 3 minutes of MIDI ≈ 3 kB;   a WAV file stores 1 minute in about 10 MB
CD-quality WAV, 1 min: 44,100 samples/s × 2 bytes × 2 channels × 60 s = 10,584,000 B ≈ 10.6 MB
3 min: 31,752,000 B ≈ 31.75 MB;  31,752,000 / 3,000 ≈ 10,584  → MIDI is about 10,000 times smaller
  • Slide 18: "Music is organized into tracks in a sequencer. Each track can be turned on or off on recording or playing back."
  • "MIDI channels are used to separate messages. There are 16 channels, numbered from 0 to 15." The channel forms "the last four bits (the least significant bits)" of the status byte.
  • Each channel is usually tied to one instrument, "for example, channel 1 is the piano, channel 10 is the drums". You can switch instruments midstream.
  • Slide 19: channel messages include a channel number. System messages are general messages for all instruments, for example "indicating a change in tuning or timing".
  • An instrument responds "by simply ignoring any "play sound" message that is not for its channel". If several notes arrive on its channel, it plays them all "provided it is multi-voice", able to play more than a single note at once.

9. MIDI bytes, the envelope and sequencers (slides 25–30)

  • Slide 25: "The data in a MIDI status byte is between 128 and 255; each of the data bytes is between 0 and 127." 128 = 1000 0000 is the first byte with its top bit set, so the top bit alone tells a status byte from a data byte.
  • "Actual MIDI bytes are 8 bit, plus a 0 start and stop bit, making them 10-bit "bytes"." On the wire every byte costs 10 bits.
  • Fig. 6.9 is the MIDI data stream. A typical message is {status byte, data byte, data byte} = {Note On, Note Number, Note Velocity}.
  • A MIDI device is often programmable. It has filters for changing the bass and treble response, and it can change the "envelope", which describes "how the amplitude of a sound changes over time".
  • Fig. 6.10 models a digital instrument's response to Note On and Note Off messages: attack, decay, sustain, release (ADSR).
  • Slide 27: sequencers (editors) let you work in standard music notation or directly in the data. "MIDI files can also store wave table data."
  • A sampler samples audio. A "drum machine" always stores wave table data of real drums. So one track can be MIDI and another digital audio, such as vocals.
  • Slide 28: MIDI is "a full messaging protocol that can be used for whatever one likes, for example for controlling lights in a theater". One message type sends "any kind and any number of bytes".
  • Sequencer tricks: looping over a few bars; volume controlled over time, "time varying amplitude modulation"; and "time compression or expansion with no pitch change".
  • Slide 29: a large change of key makes a sampled instrument sound displeasing, so samplers use multisampling. "A sound is recorded using several bandpass filters, and the resulting recordings are assigned to different keyboard keys", so each note needs less shift.
  • Slide 30: "MIDI Machine Control (MMC) is a subset of the MIDI specification that can be used for controlling recording equipment, e.g. multitrack recorders." Its most common use is to "press "Play" on a remote device", for example to start a device with a poor timer at just the right time.

10. MIDI hardware: 31.25 kbps, IN, OUT and THRU (slides 31–34)

  • "The MIDI hardware setup consists of a 31.25 kbps serial connection, with the 10-bit bytes including a 0 start and stop bit." Serial means one bit after another on one wire. "Usually, MIDI-capable units are either input devices or output devices, not both."
  • Fig. 6.11, a synthesizer: "The modulation wheel adds vibrato. Pitch bend alters the frequency, much like pulling a guitar string over slightly."
  • The ports are 5-pin connectors. "MIDI IN is the connector via which the device receives all MIDI data. MIDI OUT is the connector through which the device transmits all the MIDI data it generates itself." A third connector, THRU, "simply copies data entering the IN channel".
  • "MIDI communication is half-duplex": data goes one way at a time (textbook context for the term).

12. General MIDI and MIDI-to-WAV conversion (slides 40–41)

  • Goal of General MIDI: the same patch numbers mean the same instruments on every machine, so "patch 1 should always be a piano, not a flugelhorn".
  • "A standard percussion map also specifies 47 percussion sounds." Where a "note" appears on the score decides which percussion element is struck.
  • A General MIDI device "must support all 16 channels; must be multi-timbral (i.e., each channel can play a different instrument/program); must be polyphonic (i.e., each channel is able to play many voices); and must have a minimum of 24 dynamically allocated voices".
  • MIDI to WAV (slide 41): early versions of Adobe Premiere accept only WAV files. Shareware converters "consist of large lookup files" that substitute predefined or shifted WAV output for MIDI messages, "with inconsistent success".

13. Coding of audio and PCM (slides 42–44)

  • Slide 42: "Quantization and transformation of data are collectively known as coding of the data."
  • For audio, the μ-law technique for companding is usually combined with a simple algorithm that exploits the temporal redundancy in audio signals: neighbouring samples are similar. The slide glosses companding as "reduce the SNR of (a signal) using a compander".
  • A compander is a compressor plus an expander (textbook context). The compressor boosts quiet parts before uniform quantization and the expander undoes it, so small amplitudes get finer steps (the logarithmic quantization of Lecture 2).
  • PCM is Pulse Code Modulation (slide 2 writes "PCM code modulation").

Mistakes that cost marks

  • Using 10 log₁₀ on a voltage ratio. 100 times the voltage is 20 log₁₀ 100 = 40 dB, not 20 dB. Use 10 log₁₀ only for a power ratio.
  • Rounding the number of bits down. 50 / 6.02 = 8.31 needs 9 bits, because 8 bits give only 48.16 dB.
  • Giving the SQNR when the gain is asked. Going from 8 to 12 bits adds 4 × 6.02 = 24.08 dB; 48.16 dB is the 8-bit SQNR itself.
  • Mixing kbps and kB/s. CD audio is 1,411.2 kbps = 176.4 kB/s, and Table 6.2's column is in kilobytes. Forgetting the channels halves a stereo rate.
  • Putting the channel in the high bits. The status byte is 1ttt nnnn: flag, three message bits, four channel bits. 0 to 15 is the channel, 128 to 255 a status byte, 0 to 127 a data byte.
  • Timing MIDI with 8-bit bytes. On the wire a byte is 10 bits, so a Note On is 30 bits = 0.96 ms at 31.25 kbps, not 24 bits.
  • Mixing up the PCM rows. The expander, the DAC and the low-pass filter belong to the decoder. The encoder filters first, then compresses, then quantizes.
  • Swapping polyphony and multi-timbral. Polyphony counts voices (notes) at once; multi-timbral counts different instruments at once.

Cheat sheet

How to write it on the exam

SNR (definition + both forms)
  1. SNR = the ratio of the power of the correct signal to the noise, in dB.
  2. Power ∝ V², so voltages → 20 log₁₀(Vs / Vn), powers → 10 log₁₀(Ps / Pn).
  3. 5 V and 0.25 V:    20 log₁₀(5 / 0.25)   = 20 log₁₀ 20   = 26.02 dB
  4. 2 W and 0.5 mW:    10 log₁₀(2 / 0.0005) = 10 log₁₀ 4000 = 36.02 dB

SQNR and bits
  1. SQNR = 20 log₁₀(2^(N−1) / ½) = 20 N log₁₀ 2 = 6.02N dB; error ≤ half an interval.
  2. Target 50 dB: N ≥ 50 / 6.02 = 8.31 → round up → N = 9.
  3. Check: 8 bits → 48.16 dB < 50;  9 bits → 54.18 dB ≥ 50.
  4. Levels 2⁹ = 512;  step = 2 V / 512 = 3.91 mV;  largest error = step / 2 = 1.95 mV.

Bitrate and file size
  fₛ × bits × channels = 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bit/s = 1,411.2 kbps
  ÷ 8 = 176,400 B/s = 176.4 kB/s;   × 180 s = 31,752,000 B ≈ 31.75 MB

MIDI message
  1. Define: MIDI = protocol (early 1980s) that codes events, not samples.
  2. Table 6.3: Note On = &H9n, then key number, then velocity.
  3. Status 1001 0101 = &H95 = 149: 1 = status byte, 001 = Note On, 0101 = channel 5.
  4. Data: 67 = &H43 = 0100 0011;  90 = &H5A = 0101 1010  (MSB 0, values 0 to 127).
  5. Time: 3 bytes × 10 bits = 30 bits;  30 / 31,250 = 0.00096 s = 0.96 ms.

PCM block diagram
  encoder: bandlimiting filter → μ-law / A-law compressor → linear PCM → transmission
  decoder: DAC → μ-law / A-law expander → low-pass filter (same cutoff as the bandpass top)
Exam focus
  • SNR: 20 log₁₀ for voltages, 10 log₁₀ for powers. 100 × the voltage = 40 dB; 5 V / 0.25 V = 26.02 dB; 2 W / 0.5 mW = 36.02 dB.
  • Table 1: +10 dB = 10 × intensity, +20 dB = 10 × amplitude. Busy street 70 − average room 40 = 30 dB = 1,000 × the intensity.
  • Quantization: 2N levels, step = range / 2N, error at most half a step. 0 to 1 V with 8 bits: 256 levels, 3.9 mV step, 1.95 mV error.
  • SQNR = 20 log₁₀(2N−1 / ½) = 6.02N dB: 8 bits 48.16, 12 bits 72.24, 16 bits 96 dB. 8 → 12 bits = +24.08 dB. Realistic: 6.02N + 1.76.
  • Bits for a target T: N = ⌈T / 6.02⌉. 50 dB → 9 bits, 512 levels; 90 dB → 15 bits.
  • Bitrate = fₛ × bits × channels: CD 1,411.2 kbps = 176.4 kB/s; telephone 64 kbps = 8 kB/s; stereo doubles.
  • Speech 50 Hz to 10 kHz, music 20 Hz to 20 kHz, by a bandpass (band-limiting) filter; the decoder's low-pass filter after the DA uses the same upper cutoff.
  • MIDI codes events: 3 minutes ≈ 3 kB against 31.75 MB of CD-quality WAV, about 10,000 times smaller.
  • Status byte 1ttt nnnn, 128 to 255; data byte 0ddddddd, 0 to 127; 16 channels 0 to 15 in the low four bits. Note On channel 5 = &H95 = 1001 0101.
  • 31.25 kbps, 10-bit bytes: a 3-byte message = 30 bits = 0.96 ms; a ten-note chord 9.6 ms, 19.2 ms with the Note Offs.
  • Words: voice, polyphony, multi-timbral, patch, bank; General MIDI = 128 patches, channel 10 percussion, 47 percussion sounds, 16 channels, at least 24 voices. IN, OUT, THRU, half-duplex. ADSR.
  • PCM: transformation, loss (quantization), coding. Encoder: bandlimiting filter → μ-law / A-law compressor → linear PCM. Decoder: DAC → expander → low-pass filter.
