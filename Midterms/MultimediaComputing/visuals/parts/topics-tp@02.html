Sound and Sampling from Zero

Sound is air pressure that changes smoothly over time. A computer can only store a list of numbers, so the pressure is measured at evenly spaced moments (sampling) and each measurement is rounded to one of a fixed set of levels (quantization). This lecture explains both steps, how many bits per second they produce, and the Nyquist rule for how fast to sample before false frequencies (aliasing) appear. You need only sine waves and powers of 2 from school.

Words you need

Signal x(t)

A quantity that changes over time, such as the voltage from a microphone. x(t) is its value at time t.

Analog signal

"Represented by a continuous function": it has a value at every instant, and the value can be anything in its range.

Digital signal

A list of numbers, one per evenly spaced instant, each taken from a finite set of values.

Frequency f, period T

f is cycles per second, in hertz (Hz); 1 kHz = 1,000 Hz. T is the time of one cycle, T = 1/f, in seconds.

Sampling, fs

Measuring x(t) once every T seconds. The sampling frequency fs = 1/T counts samples per second.

Quantization, b bits

Rounding each sample to the nearest of 2b levels. b is the number of bits per sample.

Bit rate

Bits produced per second (bps): fs × bits per sample × number of channels.

Spectrum

The frequency-domain picture of a signal: one spike per sine wave, placed at its frequency, as tall as its amplitude.

Nyquist sampling frequency

2 × fmax, twice the highest frequency in the signal. The sampling rate must be greater than it.

Aliasing

"Loss of information during digitization." A sampler that is too slow records a false, lower frequency.

1. Sound is a pressure wave (slides 3–4)

Before anything is digital, sound is a physical event in the air.

  • "Sound is a wave phenomenon like light, but it is macroscopic and involves molecules of air being compressed and expanded under the action of some physical device."
  • A speaker cone moves back and forth. It pushes air molecules together (a compression, high pressure), then pulls them apart (an expansion, low pressure). The pattern travels outward as a longitudinal pressure wave: the molecules move back and forth along the direction the wave travels.
  • "Without air there is no sound." In space there is nothing to compress.
  • Pressure can take any value, so sound "takes on continuous values". It is an analog signal.
  • It still behaves like any wave: reflection (bouncing), refraction (change of angle when entering a medium with a different density) and diffraction (bending around an obstacle). This makes "surround sound" possible.
  • A transducer (a microphone) measures the pressure at one point and converts "pressure to voltage levels". From here on, the signal x(t) is that voltage over time.

2. Analog and digital signals (slides 5–11)

  • Multimedia systems have "three major components": a) content creation, b) compression and storage, c) delivery or distribution. This lecture is about a): recording each medium in digital form, so different media are easy to combine.
  • Digitizing is one-dimensional for audio, two-dimensional for images and three-dimensional for video.
  • Analog: "A signal is analog if it can be represented by a continuous function", for example amplitude against time.
  • Digital: "represented by a discrete set of values defined at specific (and most often regular) instances of the input domain, which might be time, space, or both" (Fig. 2-1).
  • Periodic analog signals are simple or composite. "A simple periodic analog signal, a sine wave, cannot be decomposed into simpler signals." A composite one "is composed of multiple sine waves".

3. Complex signals and audio sampling rates (slides 12–13)

Real sounds are not single sine waves. Slide 12 builds a complex signal by adding sine waves together (superposition): a fundamental frequency f, plus weaker waves at 2, 3, 4 and 5 times f.

  • "Using measurements only at evenly spaced time intervals is simply called sampling, and the rate at which it is performed is called the sampling frequency."
  • For audio, "typical sampling rates are from 8 kHz (8,000 samples per second) to 48 kHz".
  • The human ear hears from about 20 Hz ("a very deep rumble") to about 20 kHz. Above that is ultrasound.
  • The human voice reaches about 4 kHz. These two limits set the sampling rates of section 11.

  • "The conversion of signals from analog to digital occurs via two main processes: sampling and quantization." The reverse, digital to analog, "is known as interpolation".
  • The goal is that "no artifacts are created in the digital data", so the signal converted back "will look the same as the original analog signal".

  • "Frequency and period are the inverse of each other." Slide 18: 12 cycles in 1 s is 12 Hz, so T = 1/12 s = 83.3 ms; 6 cycles in 1 s is 6 Hz, T = 166.7 ms.
  • "Change in a short span of time means high frequency." Change over a long span of time means low frequency.
  • "If a signal does not change at all, its frequency is zero. If a signal changes instantaneously, its frequency is infinite."
  • "Phase describes the position of the waveform relative to time 0." One cycle is 360° = 2π rad. A phase of 90° = π/2 rad moves the wave by a quarter period, T/4. A phase of 180° = π rad moves it by half a period, T/2 (slide 24).

6. Time domain and frequency domain (slides 27–28)

  • A time-domain plot shows amplitude against time. A frequency-domain plot shows amplitude against frequency.
  • "A complete sine wave in the time domain can be represented by one single spike in the frequency domain." Slide 27: a sine wave with peak value 5 V and frequency 6 Hz is one spike of height 5 at 6 Hz.
  • "The frequency domain is more compact and useful when we are dealing with more than one sine wave." Slide 28: three sine waves with frequencies 0, 8 and 16 Hz and amplitudes 15, 10 and 5 are three spikes.
  • A wave of 0 Hz never changes. It is a constant level, here 15 V.

7. Quantization: levels, step and error (slides 29–33)

  • "Quantization deals with encoding the signal value at every sampled location with a predefined precision, defined by a number of levels."
  • "The entire range R of the signal is represented by a finite number of bits b." b bits can write 2b different codes (000 to 111 for b = 3), so there are N = 2b levels.
  • The levels share the range evenly. The quantization step is δ = R/2b. This is the professor's form; do not write R/(2b − 1).
  • Q is a rounding function. It maps each sample to the nearest digital value that b bits can write.

xq(n) = Q[xs(n)] N = 2b levels δ = R / 2b

R = 0 to 8 V,  b = 3   →   N = 2³ = 8 levels,  δ = 8 / 2³ = 1 V

sample xs   xs / δ   nearest level xq   code   error
  0.2 V       0.2      0 V                 000    0.2 V
  2.6 V       2.6      3 V                 011    0.4 V
  5.3 V       5.3      5 V                 101    0.3 V
  6.8 V       6.8      7 V                 111    0.2 V
  3.9 V       3.9      4 V                 100    0.1 V
  • "Because each sample is represented by a finite number of bits, the quantized value will differ from the actual signal value, thus always introducing an error." Rounding to the nearest level keeps it at most δ/2 (textbook context).
  • How many bits depends "on the type of signal and what its intended use is". "Audio signals, which represent music, must be quantized on 16 bits, whereas speech only requires 8 bits."
  • Slide 30 (Fig. 2-3) shows one signal quantized with 8 bits (256 levels), 4 bits (16 levels) and 3 bits (8 levels). Slide 31's text says "4 or 2"; the figure caption is the consistent one.
  • Slide 32 (Fig. 2-4) re-quantizes an 8-bit image from 6 bits down to 1 bit per pixel. With few bits, smooth shading turns into flat bands, and 1 bit is pure black and white.
  • Uniform (linear) quantization: "the output range of the signal is divided into fixed and uniformly separated intervals". It "works well when all the values in the range of the signal are equally likely", so the error is spread evenly.
  • Nonlinear quantization (slide 33, Fig. 2-5) uses "a logarithmically quantized interval scale". (textbook context) Its steps are finer for quiet, small values; Lecture 3 continues with it.

8. Bit rate and file size (slides 34–35)

  • Bit rate is "the number of bits being produced per second". It decides whether a signal fits on a disk or through a network "which might have high, low, or even varying bandwidths".
  • Each second the sampler produces fs samples, and each sample costs b bits. Stereo records two channels at every instant, so its rate is twice the mono rate (Fig. 2-6).
  • File size: bit rate × duration gives bits. Divide by 8 for bytes. Here k = 1,000 and M = 1,000,000.

bit rate = (samples / second) × (bits / sample) × channels = fs × b × channels (bps)

file size = bit rate × seconds / 8 (bytes)

9. Signals and systems (slides 36–41)

  • Fig. 2-7 shows signals that are continuous and smooth (a sinusoid), continuous but not smooth (a saw tooth), neither smooth nor continuous (a step edge), of finite support, and periodic. Real signals combine several of these properties.
  • Symmetric signals are odd, like y = sin(x), or even, like y = cos(x). Any signal is the sum of an odd part and an even part.
  • Finite support: the signal is defined on a finite interval and is zero outside it.
  • Periodic with period T: f(x + T) = f(x). Any f(t) gives a periodic version g(t) = Σk f(t − kT).
  • "Any operation that transforms a signal is called a system." A system turns an input x(t) into an output y(t).
  • Linear (slide 38): if x(t) = c₁x₁(t) + c₂x₂(t), then y(t) = c₁y₁(t) + c₂y₂(t), where yk(t) is the output for xk(t) alone. Scale and add the inputs, and the outputs scale and add the same way.
  • Time invariant (textbook context): delay the input by t₀, and the output is the same, delayed by t₀. LTI = linear and time invariant.
  • Convolution f*g (slide 39) is "the integral of the first signal multiplied with the other signal reversed and shifted".

(f*g)(t) = ∫ f(τ) g(t − τ) dτ = ∫ f(t − τ) g(τ) dτ (both integrals from −∞ to ∞)

discrete version (textbook context): y[n] = Σ f[k]·g[n − k]
f = [1, 2, 3],  g = [1, 1]   reverse g, shift by n, multiply, add
y[0] = 1·1 = 1    y[1] = 1·1 + 2·1 = 3    y[2] = 2·1 + 3·1 = 5    y[3] = 3·1 = 3
f * g = [1, 3, 5, 3]
  • "Any LTI system is fully characterized by a specific function, which is called the impulse response of the system." (textbook context) It is the output when the input is a delta, the single spike of section 10.
  • "The output of the system is the convolution of the input with the system's impulse response": y(t) = h(t) * x(t). This is "the time domain point of view".
  • "The transfer function is the Fourier transform of the system's impulse response." In the frequency domain the convolution becomes a multiplication, Y(s) = H(s) × X(s) (Fig. 2-8, slide 41).
time domain        x(t) ──► [ h(t) ] ──► y(t) = h(t) * x(t)      convolution
                     │ FT        │ FT          ▲ inverse FT

frequency domain   X(s) ──► [ H(s) ] ──► Y(s) = H(s) × X(s)      multiplication

10. Useful signals and the Fourier transform (slides 42–44, 49)

  • The delta, box and step functions "play an important role in many signaling-processing operations, such as sampling, filtering, and convolution".
  • The Dirac delta δ(x), introduced by the physicist Paul Dirac, is "a sharp peak bounding a unit area". It is zero everywhere except at x = 0, where it becomes infinite, and its integral is 1.

  • The Fourier transform "is due to Joseph Fourier (1768–1830), who published his initial results in 1807".
  • "Fourier proposed to represent any periodic, continuous signal as a sum of individual complex sinusoids (a Fourier series expansion)." That is a weighted combination of sine and cosine waves, the recipe built by hand in Figure 1.
  • The weights "are called the Fourier series coefficients, or spectral components". They are the spike heights of the frequency domain (section 6).
  • Fig. 2-11 (slide 49): the Fourier transforms "show the amount of energy at all frequencies". Two signals of one frequency each give one spike each, and the lower-frequency signal's spike sits further left.

11. The sampling theorem (slides 45–48)

  • Fig. 2-10 (slides 45–46): a signal that never changes (zero frequency) needs "one single sample". A straight ramp needs two. A wiggly signal needs many more. "As we go from left to right, the frequency content in the signals increases", and so does the number of samples needed.
  • The rule "was established during the late 1920s by Henry Nyquist and later formalized by Claude Shannon in 1950".
  • "The signal has to be sampled using a sampling frequency that is greater than twice the maximal frequency occurring in the signal."

fs > 2 fmax Nyquist sampling frequency = 2 fmax highest frequency kept < fs/2

  • Formal statement (slide 48): a band-limited signal with highest frequency ωF is fully determined by its samples f(nT) if 2π/T > 2ωF, the angle form of fs > 2fmax. It can be rebuilt from the samples by convolution with a sinc filter.
  • Slide example: "if the signal has a maximal frequency of 10 kHz, it should be sampled at a frequency greater than 20 kHz. Here, 20 kHz is known as the Nyquist sampling frequency for that signal."
  • Voice reaches about 4 kHz, and 2 × 4 = 8 kHz is the speech rate of Fig. 2-6. Hearing reaches 20 kHz, so music needs more than 40 kHz, and the audio CD uses 44.1 kHz.
  • Read the rule the other way round: a recording made at fs holds frequencies only below fs/2. The CD keeps everything below 22.05 kHz.
  • The professor's "Nyquist frequency" (slides 48, 50, 51) is 2fmax, the minimum sampling rate. (textbook context) Many books call 2fmax the Nyquist rate and use "Nyquist frequency" for fs/2. On this exam, use the slide's meaning and say which one you mean.

12. Oversampling, undersampling and aliasing (slides 50–51)

  • Sampling above the Nyquist frequency (oversampling) is "nothing special". The signal "is guaranteed to have all the necessary frequencies", so the same signal is reproduced. The cost: "more digital data than necessary, which increases your storage and transmission overhead".
  • Sampling below it (undersampling): "all the frequency content is not well captured", so the signal heard, viewed or converted back "does not correspond to the initial starting analog signal".
  • "Aliasing is the term used to describe loss of information during digitization."
  • (textbook context) A tone above fs/2 does not vanish. It comes back as a false tone, the alias, at its distance from the nearest multiple of fs. For fs/2 < f < fs that is fs − f.

falias = | f − k·fs |, k = the whole number nearest to f / fs (textbook context)

13. Aliasing in space and in time (slides 52–55)

  • "Aliasing effects in the spatial domain are seen in all dimensions." The 1D example of Fig. 2-12 shows the original signal, the sampled points and the reconstructed signal, which comes out at a much lower frequency.
  • The 2D example starts from an image of "750 samples in the x and 620 samples in the y direction" and rebuilds it from fewer samples in both directions. "As the number of spatial samples decreases, not all of the original frequencies are properly captured": fine detail turns into blocks and false patterns.
  • Halving the resolution in both directions keeps 375 × 310 = 116,250 of the 750 × 620 = 465,000 samples, one quarter. Image resampling returns in Lecture 6.
  • Temporal aliasing (slide 55): in western movies the stagecoach wheels first turn forward, then, as the coach speeds up, "appear to rotate in the opposite direction". "This occurs because the movie camera has under sampled the wheels' motion."

14. Halving the sample rate (slides 19, 34, 47–51 applied)

A common task: keep every second sample, x′(n) = xs(2n). The period T doubles and fs halves. Every other quantity follows from a formula above. Example: a 10 s stereo recording at 44.1 kHz with 16 bits per sample.

  • Content between the new limit, 11.025 kHz, and the old one, 22.05 kHz, is now undersampled and aliases. A 15 kHz tone comes back at 22.05 − 15 = 7.05 kHz. A 5 kHz tone stays at 5 kHz.
  • (textbook context) To avoid this, low-pass filter the signal below the new fs/2 before dropping samples, and write the new rate into the file header. The duration stays the same and the file is half the size.

Mistakes that cost marks

  • Nyquist sampling frequency = 2 × fmax. fmax itself and fmax/2 are wrong, and sampling at exactly 2fmax is not enough: the rule says greater than.
  • b bits give 2b levels. b levels and 2b levels are wrong. The step is δ = R/2b in this course.
  • Bit rate needs every factor: fs × bits × channels. Stereo doubles it. File size in bytes divides the bits by 8, and 1 MB = 106 bytes unless the question says otherwise.
  • Phase to time: Δt = (φ/360°) × T. 90° is T/4 and 180° is T/2. Find T = 1/f first.
  • Wavelength λ = v/f = vT, in metres. f/v and v × f are wrong, and T (in seconds) is a time, not a wavelength.
  • An undersampled tone does not disappear. It comes back at a lower, false frequency, fs − f for fs/2 < f < fs.
  • Halving fs halves the bit rate, the file size and the highest frequency kept. The bits per sample stay the same.
  • Oversampling does not improve the signal. It reproduces the same signal with more data, so storage and transmission cost more.

How to write it on the exam

A. "Explain sampling and quantization. What is the difference?"
   Sampling: measurements "at evenly spaced time intervals"; fs = 1/T; xs(n) = x(nT).   time axis
   Quantization: each sample rounded to the nearest of 2b levels; δ = R/2b;
                 always introduces an error (at most δ/2).                        amplitude axis
   A/D = sampling + quantization.  D/A = interpolation.

B. "Highest frequency the recording can hold? Enough for hearing?"
   fs > 2fmax  →  fmax < fs/2 = ...
   compare: hearing 20 kHz needs fs > 40 kHz; voice 4 kHz needs fs > 8 kHz
   if too low: content above fs/2 aliases

C. "Nyquist sampling frequency" = 2 × fmax   (fs must be greater than it)

D. "At what frequency does each tone appear?"
   f < fs/2  →  unchanged
   f > fs/2  →  | f − k·fs |, nearest multiple; fs − f when fs/2 < f < fs

E. "Bit rate and file size"
   bit rate = fs × bits × channels = ... bps
   size     = bit rate × seconds / 8 = ... bytes   (/ 106 for MB)

F. "Give A, f, T, φ and λ of A sin(2πft + φ)"
   A in V;  f in Hz;  T = 1/f;  φ in rad and in degrees;  Δt = (φ/360°) × T;  λ = v/f = vT

G. "The sample rate is halved"
   new fs, new limit fs/2, bit rate / 2, size / 2, bits per sample unchanged;
   content between the new and the old limit aliases unless low-pass filtered first
Exam focus
  • Definitions: sampling = time, quantization = amplitude, A/D = both, D/A = interpolation, aliasing = "loss of information during digitization".
  • T = 1/f: 12 Hz → 83.3 ms. Phase 90° = π/2 rad = T/4; at 100 Hz that is 2.5 ms.
  • λ = v/f: 1 kHz in air (340 m/s) → 0.34 m.
  • fs > 2fmax: 10 kHz → more than 20 kHz (the Nyquist sampling frequency); voice 4 kHz → 8 kHz; hearing 20 kHz → more than 40 kHz (CD 44.1 kHz).
  • Alias (textbook context): 6 kHz sampled at 8 kHz is heard at 2 kHz.
  • Levels 2b, step R/2b: R = 8 V, b = 3 → 8 levels, δ = 1 V. Music 16 bits, speech 8 bits.
  • Bit rate: CD 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bps; one minute = 10,584,000 bytes. HDTV frame 1920 × 1080 × 12 = 24.88 Mbit.
  • Frequency domain: a 5 V, 6 Hz sine is one spike of height 5 at 6 Hz; slide 28 is three spikes, 15, 10 and 5 at 0, 8 and 16 Hz.
  • LTI: y = h * x, Y = H × X, the transfer function is the Fourier transform of the impulse response.
